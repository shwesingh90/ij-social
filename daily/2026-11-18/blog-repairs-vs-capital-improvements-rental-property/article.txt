Repairs vs Capital Improvements Rental Property | InvestorJoint + Tax · 16 Nov 2026 · 9 min read Repairs vs Capital Improvements on a Rental Property The difference between a repair and a capital improvement determines whether you deduct $8,000 this year or spread it over 40. Here's how the ATO draws the line—and why getting it wrong costs you. The line between a repair and a capital improvement is worth tens of thousands of dollars over the life of an investment property. Replace a broken hot water system with an identical model? That's typically a repair—claim the full $2,400 this financial year. Upgrade to a solar-boosted system with a larger tank? That's a capital improvement—depreciate the cost over 40 years as part of the building's plant and equipment schedule. The ATO's position is clear in principle but messy in practice, and the distinction matters most when cashflow is tight and you're deciding whether to defer or accelerate work. The Core Test: Repairs Restore, Improvements Enhance The ATO applies a function-over-form test. A repair restores a worn or broken asset to its original condition without materially improving it. A capital improvement creates something better, bigger, or fundamentally different—enhancing the property's income-earning capacity, structural integrity, or market value. In FCT v. Lincoln (1979), the High Court established that replacing part of an asset with a modern equivalent doesn't automatically make it an improvement. The question is whether the replacement is "of the same or equivalent character" and whether it merely restores functionality or materially betters the property. That ruling still anchors ATO guidance today. Practical examples from Taxation Ruling TR 97/23: Repair : Replacing cracked roof tiles with identical tiles; repainting faded exterior walls; patching carpet holes; fixing a leaking tap. Capital improvement : Extending the roofline to cover a new carport; rendering and painting bare brick for the first time; replacing carpet with timber floorboards; installing a second bathroom. The grey zone sits in replacements. If you replace a white ceiling fan with a brushed-nickel model featuring LED downlights, the ATO may argue the decorative and functional upgrade pushes it into capital territory—even if the old fan was broken. Initial Repairs: The Black Hole for New Purchasers When you buy an established rental property, initial repairs —work done to fix defects that existed at purchase—are not immediately deductible. They're also not depreciable as capital works. They sit in a tax black hole. The ATO's position, confirmed in cases like FCT v. Thomas (1972) and codified in TR 97/23, is that initial repairs do not relate to income production because the property was not yet income-producing when the defect arose. You bought the property with the defect; fixing it is part of putting the asset into a working state, not maintaining it. Examples of initial repairs: Replacing a cracked vanity basin discovered during your pre-settlement inspection. Repairing broken floorboards noted in the building report. Fixing a leaking shower that the vendor disclosed but didn't remedy. Even if you complete the work after settlement and the property is tenanted, the defect pre-dated income production. No deduction. One critical exception: If the property was income-producing before you purchased it—say, you bought it with an existing tenant in place and the defect arose after settlement—you can claim it as a repair. The ATO distinguishes between defects you inherit and defects that emerge while you are earning rent. This is why buyers often negotiate price reductions rather than requiring the vendor to fix issues. A $5,000 discount on purchase price increases your cost base (reducing future CGT) and may allow you to claim some remedial work as a capital improvement if it's substantial enough. But it won't give you an immediate deduction. Replacements: When Like-for-Like Becomes Capital Replacing a broken asset with a modern equivalent is usually a repair— if you're replacing a discrete item of plant and equipment (hot water, oven, dishwasher, air conditioner) and the new item is functionally similar. Immediate deduction as repair: Gas storage hot water (170 L) replaced with gas instantaneous unit—similar capacity, same fuel type. Broken oven replaced with a new oven of equivalent size and features. Cracked toilet cistern replaced with a standard close-coupled suite. Capital improvement requiring depreciation: Electric hot water replaced with solar-boosted heat pump system—step-change in efficiency and cost. Free-standing oven replaced with built-in wall oven plus separate cooktop—structural alteration. Standard toilet replaced with in-wall cistern requiring tile removal and plumbing relocation. The dividing line often comes down to cost and scope. If the replacement requires structural work (moving walls, re-plumbing, rewiring) or materially upgrades the property's appeal to tenants, the ATO leans toward capital treatment. Entire-asset replacement is a separate trap. If you replace an entire roof structure (not just tiles), or gut and rebuild a kitchen (not just swap an oven), you've crossed into capital works. The fact that the old asset was worn out doesn't convert a major reconstruction into a repair. Deduction Timing: Immediate vs Depreciation Repairs: Section 25-10 ITAA 1997 Fully deductible in the year incurred, provided the property was income-producing when the expense was incurred. Claim the full invoice amount on your tax return—painter, plumber, materials, the lot. If you pay $4,800 to repaint the interior in June 2025, that's $4,800 off your FY25 assessable income (assuming the work is complete or you're on an accruals basis). Capital Improvements: Division 43 and Division 40 Structural improvements (extensions, renovations, in-ground pools, sealed driveways): depreciate at 2.5 % per year under Division 43 capital works. A $100,000 renovation = $2,500 deduction per year for 40 years (if construction began after 15 September 1987 and the property is income-producing). Plant and equipment (air con, blinds, hot water): depreciate under Division 40 using ATO effective-life tables. A $3,500 split-system might depreciate at 10 % (diminishing value), giving you $350 in year one, $315 in year two, and so on. You cannot deduct the purchase cost up-front. You must obtain a depreciation schedule from a quantity surveyor (typically $600–$900) to claim the deductions correctly. The depreciation calculator will show you indicative annual deductions, but the ATO expects a compliant schedule. Prepayments and Staging If you pay a contractor $12,000 in June 2025 but work completes in August 2025, you generally cannot deduct the full amount in FY25 unless you're a small business taxpayer and the work is completed within the eligible service period (s 82KZMF). Most individual property investors must wait until the work is done. Common Scenarios and ATO Interpretation Painting Repair : Repainting the same colour to cover wear, sun damage, or minor marks. Immediate deduction. Capital : Painting for the first time (e.g., bare render), or painting as part of an extension or renovation. Depreciate as part of capital works. If you repaint internally every five years to keep the property tenantable, that's maintenance. If you repaint after adding a new bedroom, the cost of painting the entire home (including the extension) is capital. Kitchen and Bathroom Repair : Replacing a broken oven, tap, or cabinet door. Replacing cracked tiles (same size/colour). Capital : New benchtops, cabinetry, splashback, or layout changes. Replacing a bath with a frameless shower. Tiling over an existing laminate floor. A "kitchen refresh"—new benchtop, cabinet handles, splashback—might cost $8,000 and feel like maintenance, but it's almost always capital in the ATO's eyes because you've enhanced functionality and appeal. Flooring Repair : Patching a hole in existing carpet; sanding and resealing existing timber. Capital : Replacing carpet with timber, tile, or hybrid; laying floor covering for the first time (e.g., polished concrete in a garage conversion). Entire-floor replacement is contentious. If the old carpet is worn but serviceable and you rip it out for timber, that's an improvement. If it's stained, torn, and untenantable, replacing it with new carpet of similar quality is usually accepted as a repair—but expect the ATO to scrutinise material upgrades. Fencing and Landscaping Repair : Replacing palings in an existing Colorbond fence; repairing a broken gate latch. Capital : Erecting a new fence where none existed; replacing a wire fence with Colorbond; landscaping a bare yard (turf, garden beds, retaining walls). Fencing is depreciable plant (Division 40) if it's temporary or removable; a permanent brick fence may be capital works (Division 43). The distinction matters for depreciation rates and whether second-hand buyers can claim deductions. Documentation and Evidence Because the repair-vs-capital decision is fact-specific, keep: 1. Photos before, during, and after—proving the asset was broken and you restored (not improved) it. 2. Quotes and invoices itemising labour, materials, and scope. "Kitchen renovation—$15,000" tells the ATO nothing. "Replace broken dishwasher (Bosch SMS40E08AU) with Bosch SMS46MW05A, equivalent capacity" makes the repair case. 3. Building and pest reports at purchase, showing what was defective then (initial repairs) versus what broke later. 4. Depreciation schedules updated each time you make a capital improvement. The quantity surveyor will add the new hot water system or bathroom to the schedule, generating deductions for the next decade. The ATO routinely adjusts returns in audit where taxpayers claim $20,000 "repairs" with no breakdown. If your accountant asks for detail, they're protecting you. What This Means for Your Portfolio When you hold multiple properties in different entities—personal name, family trust, SMSF—tracking repairs versus capital improvements becomes an entity-by-entity exercise. A repair claimed in your trust reduces taxable income distributed to beneficiaries this year; a capital improvement in your SMSF adds to the asset's cost base, reducing tax on the eventual 10 % (or 0 % in pension phase) CGT bill.
