Rental Property Expenses You Can Claim | InvestorJoint + Tax · 17 Nov 2026 · 9 min read Rental Property Expenses: What You Can and Cannot Claim Most landlords miss deductions or over-claim and trigger ATO scrutiny. Here's the full checklist of rental property expenses you can claim—and the traps that catch even experienced investors. The golden rule is simple: you can claim expenses to the extent they're incurred in earning rental income. That means immediately deductible costs (interest, rates, repairs) and capital works spread over decades (building depreciation). It also means the ATO will reject claims for pre-income vacant land, private travel disguised as inspections, and capital improvements treated as repairs. Get this right and you unlock thousands of dollars a year; get it wrong and you'll pay penalties on top of the shortfall. Immediately Deductible Expenses These hit your tax return in full in the year you pay them, reducing assessable income dollar-for-dollar. Loan interest – The largest line item for most leveraged investors. Deduct interest on loans used to purchase the property, refinance, or renovate if those works are deductible repairs or capital works. If you redrew equity for private purposes—a holiday, shares outside a complying structure—apportion the interest. The ATO's TR 2023/2 confirms apportionment applies from the moment funds are mixed. Council and water rates – Fully deductible. This includes municipal rates, water and sewerage charges, land tax, and emergency services levies. If you own interstate, state land tax still applies (NSW, VIC, QLD all charge it separately). Property management and letting fees – Agent commissions, tenant-finding fees, advertising on realestate.com.au or Domain, tenant reference checks, and ongoing management retainers. Even a fixed letting fee for a 12-month lease is deductible upfront if you're a cash-basis taxpayer (most individuals are). Insurance – Landlord insurance, building insurance, contents cover for furnished rentals, and public liability. Income-protection insurance for lost rent is deductible; mortgage protection insurance is not (it's a capital policy linked to loan discharge, not income). Repairs and maintenance – This is the most audited category. You can claim work that restores the property to its former condition—replacing broken tiles, fixing a leaking tap, repainting a scuffed wall in the same colour. You cannot claim initial repairs (fixing damage that existed when you bought), improvements (adding a deck, renovating a bathroom), or betterments (upgrading from laminate to stone benchtops). Those are capital and either depreciated or added to your cost base. For a deep dive on the line between repair and improvement, see ATO TR 97/23 . Gardening and cleaning – Mowing, edging, gutter clears, pest control, and end-of-lease cleans when deducted from the bond and you top it back up. If you do it yourself, you can't claim your own labour—only materials (mulch, sprays, bin bags). Strata levies – Both the administrative fund and sinking fund are deductible in the year levied, even though sinking-fund dollars sit untouched until the owners' corporation calls them for capital works. The ATO treats them as an outgoing incurred to maintain your rental income stream. Legal and accounting fees – Deduct the cost of preparing the rental schedule in your tax return, tenancy-tribunal representation, eviction proceedings, and ongoing advice about deductions. You cannot deduct conveyancing or solicitor fees when you acquire the property (those are added to cost base). Structuring advice—setting up a discretionary trust or unit trust—is also capital and must be amortised over five years under the blackhole-expenditure rules (Div 40-880 ITAA 1997). Bank charges and transaction fees – Account-keeping fees on the offset or loan account, discharge fees when refinancing (but not establishment fees—see the next section), and merchant fees if tenants pay rent by credit card. Borrowing Expenses Spread Over Five Years Loan establishment costs—application fees, valuation fees, lenders' mortgage insurance (LMI), mortgage-broker commissions, and stamp duty on the mortgage (still charged in some states for mortgages over certain thresholds)—cannot be claimed upfront. They must be deducted evenly over five years or the life of the loan, whichever is shorter. For example, if you paid \$8,000 in LMI and a \$600 application fee on a 30-year loan taken out in December 2023, you claim \$1,720 in FY 2023–24, \$1,720 in each of the next three years, and \$1,720 in FY 2027–28—five claims of one-fifth. If you refinance or sell in year three, you claim the remaining balance (\$3,440) in that final year. Refinancing restarts the clock. The undeducted portion of the old loan's costs is claimed in full in the year of discharge; the new loan's costs begin a fresh five-year schedule. This rule is legislated under section 25-25 ITAA 1997 and catches many first-time investors off guard. Capital Works and Depreciation Building depreciation (Div 43) – You can claim 2.5 % per year of the construction cost for buildings completed after 15 September 1987. Residential construction costs include bricks, roof, plumbing, wiring, and built-in cupboards. The deduction runs for 40 years from completion. If you bought established, you'll need a depreciation schedule from a quantity surveyor (cost: \$450–\$800). That schedule will also identify the construction date and eligible base value. Plant and equipment (Div 40) – Hot-water systems, ovens, dishwashers, air-conditioners, carpets, blinds, and ceiling fans depreciate at prescribed effective-life rates (e.g. 10 years for a dishwasher = 10 % diminishing value). Note that since 1 July 2017, if you buy an established residential property you can only claim plant depreciation on items you purchased new. Pre-existing plant is quarantined under the "second-hand plant exclusion" (section 40-27 ITAA 1997). New builds and commercial property are exempt from this rule. Capital improvements – The ensuite you added, the carport you built, the full kitchen renovation—these increase the cost base for CGT but do not generate an immediate deduction. If the improvement is a building extension, the new structure may qualify for Div 43 from its completion date. Low-value and low-cost pooling – Assets under \$300 can be claimed outright if you choose. Assets \$300–\$1,000 can be pooled and depreciated at 18.75 % in year one, 37.5 % thereafter. Temporary full expensing (which ended 30 June 2023) no longer applies to residential rental plant. Travel Expenses—Almost Always Denied Before July 2017, landlords could claim airfares, accommodation, and meal costs when travelling to inspect a property, meet an agent, or oversee repairs. Since 1 July 2017, travel to inspect or maintain a residential rental is non-deductible under section 26-31 ITAA 1997—even if the trip's sole purpose is rental-related. Exceptions are narrow: 1. You carry on a business of property development or property letting at a scale the ATO recognises as a business (typically 10+ properties or full-time professional scale). 2. The property is commercial (retail, office, industrial), not residential. Car expenses to and from the property fall into the same black hole. You cannot claim the drive from home to meet the plumber or show a prospective tenant through. If you're a tradie who also owns rentals, you can't double-dip mileage for a job and an inspection. What you can still claim : the cost of a property manager driving to the property (embedded in their fee), or the postage and phone calls to coordinate repairs remotely. This is a common audit trigger. The ATO data-matches flight bookings and cross-references landlord addresses with investment-property locations. Claiming \$1,200 in flights to the Gold Coast when you live in Melbourne will draw a please-explain. Vacant Land and Pre-Income Periods You cannot deduct holding costs—interest, rates, land tax—on vacant land unless it is genuinely held for the purpose of earning assessable income now . Holding land while waiting for a rezone, planning approval, or future development = no deduction. The intent to build a rental "one day" is insufficient; the ATO wants evidence the land is currently income-producing. When you can claim : The land has a dwelling and you're genuinely trying to lease it (advertising, agent engaged), even if it sits vacant between tenants. You've started construction of a rental dwelling and the work is continuous (the ATO accepts costs from the date construction commences to practical completion). When you cannot : Bought the block; waiting 18 months to get finance for a build. Knocked down the old house; land sat idle for a year before you lodged plans. Holding for capital growth with no active rental effort. This is clarified in TR 2004/4 (deductions for vacant land) and reinforced in dozens of AAT decisions. If you're in a grey zone, quarantine those expenses in a spreadsheet and revisit with your accountant once construction or leasing begins. What You Definitely Cannot Claim Initial repairs – Defects that existed at purchase. If the building inspection revealed a cracked slab or non-compliant wiring and you fixed it after settlement, that's an improvement to bring the property to rentable standard. It's capital. The same work, if it arose from tenant damage or wear after you started renting, would be deductible. Cost-base additions – Conveyancing, stamp duty on the transfer (not the mortgage), pest and building inspection fees. These are added to your cost base and reduce CGT when you sell. Private-use apportionment – If you let the property to your daughter at 50 % of market rent, deductions are scaled back to 50 %. If you used it yourself for a holiday, apportion expenses by days rented ÷ days available. Mortgage principal – Only the interest component is deductible; principal repayments are a reduction of your loan liability, not an outgoing incurred in earning income. Decline in value of land – Land does not depreciate (it's not a wasting asset), so there's no Div 40 deduction for land. Only buildings and plant depreciate. Body-corporate special levies for improvements – If the owners' corporation levies \$20,000 for a new pool, that's a capital improvement. It's not deductible; it's added to your cost base. Levies for repairs and maintenance are deductible. Record-Keeping and the ATO's Five-Year Window Keep every invoice, bank statement, and loan variation for five years from the date you lodge the return. The ATO's data-matching sweeps rental income from property-management software and cross-checks declared interest against loan accounts reported by lenders. Under-declaring rent or over-claiming expenses triggers a risk score that can fast-track an audit. What to keep : Loan statements showing interest, fees, and principal splits. Council and water rates notices (often annual; don't discard mid-year). Property-manager statements (monthly or quarterly) detailing rent, fees, letting costs, and repairs. Quotes and invoices for repairs vs. improvements—annotate the job scope and date so your accountant can classify correctly. Depreciation schedules and any quantity-surveyor updates after capital works. Digital workflows save hours .
