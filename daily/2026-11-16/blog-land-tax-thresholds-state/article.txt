Land Tax Thresholds by State 2024–25 | InvestorJoint + States · 13 Nov 2026 · 9 min read Land Tax by State: Thresholds, Rates and Trust Surcharges Every Australian state charges land tax differently. Understanding thresholds, trust surcharges, and aggregation rules can save you thousands each year. If you own investment property in Australia, you're almost certainly paying land tax—or will be once your portfolio crosses your state's threshold. The 2024–25 land tax thresholds range from $25,000 in Victoria to $600,000 in Tasmania, rates run anywhere from 0.165% to 3.7%, and trust surcharges can double your bill in some jurisdictions. Here's exactly what you'll pay in each state, which structures attract penalties, and how aggregation rules can catch out investors who thought they were under the threshold. How land tax works in Australia Land tax is an annual state tax levied on the total unimproved value of land you own, excluding your principal place of residence. Each state calculates the unimproved value as at midnight on 31 December (in most jurisdictions) or 30 June (South Australia, Tasmania), then applies its own progressive scale. You pay nothing if your total taxable landholdings fall below the threshold; once you cross it, tax is calculated on either the full value or the excess, depending on the state. Two design choices make land tax particularly painful for multi-property investors. First, aggregation : most states add up all land you own—across every suburb and postcode—then apply a single progressive rate schedule. If you hold three apartments each worth $400,000 of land, your $1.2 million aggregate can push you into a higher bracket than three separate owners would pay. Second, trust surcharges : Victoria, New South Wales, South Australia and Tasmania impose a flat premium—between 0.375% and 2%—on land held in discretionary and unit trusts, on the theory that trusts are used primarily for tax planning rather than occupation. The interaction of thresholds, aggregation and surcharges means the structure you choose has a direct dollar impact. A family trust holding four properties in NSW will pay both the general land tax rate and a 2% surcharge, while the same portfolio split between two individuals might stay below the threshold altogether. Trusts and absentee owners start paying at $25,000, and the trust surcharge of 0.375% (the "absentee owner surcharge" also applies to discretionary trusts at 1.5%, bringing the combined impost to 1.875%) adds meaningful cost. A discretionary trust holding land valued at $1 million will pay approximately $11,650 in land tax for 2024, compared to $8,850 for an individual—a difference of $2,800 per year. New South Wales offers a $1,075,000 threshold for 2024–25, the most generous on the eastern seaboard. Once you exceed it, the general rate is $100 + 1.6% on the excess up to $6,571,000, then 2% above that. Trusts pay an additional flat 2% surcharge on the total taxable value, meaning a trust holding $2 million of land pays approximately $16,900 general land tax plus $40,000 surcharge—$56,900 in total. NSW Revenue has confirmed that the 2% trust surcharge applies to discretionary trusts, unit trusts (unless a "fixed trust" exemption is claimed), and hybrid structures, but not to complying SMSFs or deceased estates during administration. Queensland sets different thresholds by ownership type: $600,000 for individuals, $350,000 for companies and trusts. The rate schedules also differ slightly—trusts and companies start at $1,450 + 1.7% on the excess, while individuals pay $500 + 1.65%. The top marginal rate is 2.75% above $5 million (individuals) or $2.25 million (companies/trusts). Queensland does not impose a separate surcharge, but the lower threshold and higher base fee achieve a similar result. A discretionary trust holding $1 million of land pays roughly $12,500; an individual with the same landholdings pays approximately $7,100. Western Australia has a $300,000 threshold and a convoluted rate schedule with a flat $300 base rate for land between $300,000 and $420,000, then progressive brackets that peak at 2.67% for land above $11 million. WA imposes no trust surcharge, which makes it one of the more structure-neutral jurisdictions. The Office of State Revenue revalues land annually based on data supplied by Landgate; investors should check their assessed value each year and lodge an objection within 60 days if the valuation appears inflated. South Australia expresses rates in dollars per thousand: the 2024–25 scale runs from $0.50 per $1,000 (on land between $626,000 and $1,158,000) up to $3.70 per $1,000 above $1,734,000. Trusts pay an additional 2% surcharge. SA calculates land tax on the site value as at 30 June in the preceding financial year, which can create timing mismatches if you purchase mid-year. RevenueSA applies aggregation strictly: all land held by a trust is pooled, even if different beneficiaries occupy each property. Tasmania starts charging at $25,000—the nation's lowest threshold—but applies relatively gentle marginal rates, topping out at 1.5%. Trusts pay a 0.5% surcharge. Because the threshold is so low, almost any vacant block or investment unit will trigger a land tax liability, yet the total dollar figure remains modest: a trust holding $500,000 of land pays approximately $4,850. Valuation is as at 1 July each year, and the State Revenue Office allows online objections within 60 days. Australian Capital Territory abandoned thresholds in favour of a broad-based land tax that applies to all land except a single principal place of residence. The 2024 residential rate is a fixed charge of $1,425 plus 0.63% of the average unimproved value (AUV). Because the ACT is also phasing out stamp duty in favour of higher annual rates, land tax bills are rising steadily each year. The ACT does not impose a trust surcharge, but the absence of any threshold means every investment property—no matter how small—carries an annual bill. Northern Territory does not levy land tax, making it the only jurisdiction where investors pay zero annual holding cost on land value. This is often cited as a competitive advantage for Darwin and regional NT property markets, though other factors—insurance premiums, cyclone risk, and shallow tenant demand—tend to offset the land tax saving. Trust surcharges and how they compound Four states—NSW, Victoria, South Australia and Tasmania—charge discretionary and unit trusts a premium on top of the general land tax rate. The policy rationale is that trusts allow income splitting and asset protection, so they should contribute more to revenue. In practice, the surcharge can make an otherwise tax-effective structure uneconomic for property. NSW's 2% surcharge is the bluntest instrument. It applies to the total taxable land value held by a trust, not just the amount above the threshold. A discretionary trust holding $3 million of Sydney land will pay: General land tax: $100 + 1.6% × ($3m − $1.075m) = $100 + $30,800 = $30,900 Trust surcharge: 2% × $3m = $60,000 Total: $90,900 An individual with the same portfolio pays only $30,900. The $60,000 annual penalty often exceeds the income-tax benefit of splitting rental profits among family members, particularly when negative gearing already pushes the primary investor into a low marginal bracket. For this reason, many NSW accountants now recommend holding investment property in individual names or within an SMSF (which is exempt from the surcharge) unless asset protection is paramount. Victoria's trust surcharge is smaller—0.375%—but sits atop the absentee owner surcharge of 1.5% if the trustee does not qualify for the general land tax threshold (i.e. landholdings valued at $25,000 or more trigger the higher scale). The effective combined impost for an absentee discretionary trust is 1.875%, though the State Revenue Office's interpretation of "absentee" can be murky. A trust with all Australian-resident beneficiaries is generally treated as non-absentee, but if any discretionary object is an overseas trust or foreign individual, the entire trust may be classified as absentee. This classification can add tens of thousands of dollars to the annual bill; investors should seek a private ruling from the Commissioner of State Revenue if their trust deed includes foreign discretionary objects "just in case." South Australia's 2% surcharge mirrors NSW. It applies to trusts and certain super funds (though complying SMSFs are exempt). The aggregation rule is strict: if two discretionary trusts share a common appointor or have overlapping sets of beneficiaries, RevenueSA may treat them as related and aggregate their landholdings for threshold purposes. This "related trust" test is broader than most investors expect; draft trust deeds should be reviewed by a lawyer familiar with SA land tax grouping provisions before settlement. Tasmania's 0.5% surcharge is the gentlest, but it still matters when compounded over decades. A trust holding $1 million of Hobart land pays an extra $5,000 per year, or $150,000 over a typical 30-year hold period. That foregone capital—if invested at 7% annual growth—would be worth approximately $475,000. Importantly, SMSFs are exempt from trust surcharges in every state. An SMSF holding $3 million of NSW land pays only the general land tax of $30,900, not the additional $60,000 surcharge. This is one reason high-net-worth investors often park property inside superannuation once their balance cap allows. The trade-off is that SMSFs face contribution caps, borrowing restrictions under limited recourse borrowing arrangements, and strict sole-purpose tests; see the SMSF guide for a full comparison. Aggregation rules: how multiple properties push you into higher brackets Every state except the Northern Territory aggregates land owned by the same taxpayer (or trustee) when calculating the total taxable value. If you own three properties with unimproved land values of $400,000, $500,000 and $600,000, the state adds them to $1.5 million and applies the progressive rate schedule to that sum. You do not get three separate $1,075,000 thresholds (in NSW) or three $50,000 thresholds (in Victoria). Aggregation applies within a state . If you own two apartments in Sydney (total land value $1.2 million) and one in Melbourne (land value $600,000), NSW will tax the $1.2 million and Victoria will tax the $600,000 separately. Interstate aggregation does not occur—yet. There have been periodic Treasury discussions about a national land register, but as of 2024–25 each state taxes only the land within its borders. Joint ownership complicates aggregation. If you and your spouse each own a 50% interest in two properties as tenants in common, most states will assess each of you on 50% of the combined land value. However, if you hold the properties as joint tenants—or if one partner owns one property outright and the other owns a second property outright—each taxpayer's landholdings are aggregated separately. This can be used strategically: splitting a four-property portfolio 2-and-2 between spouses may keep each person below the threshold, whereas holding all four jointly guarantees you exceed it. Company and trust grouping is more aggressive. Victoria, NSW and South Australia will aggregate land across multiple trusts if they are "commonly controlled"—typically defined as sharing the same appointor, having substantially the same beneficiaries, or being under the common direction of a single individual. The onus is on the taxpayer to demonstrate independence. If you set up three family trusts with identical beneficiary classes and your spouse as appointor of all three, revenue offices will likely treat them as a single landowner and aggregate the lot. Queensland's legislation explicitly aggregates land held by "relevant entities" in a corporate group or trust group, using definitions borrowed from the Corporations Act and common law. The Queensland Office of State Revenue has published guidance (Public Ruling DA000.1.3) setting out the tests for related trusts; broadly, if two trusts can be varied by the same person, or if income can be appointed to an overlapping set of beneficiaries, they are grouped. Aggregation also reaches related parties in some contexts. For example, if a company you control owns land, and you also own land personally, NSW may treat your personal and corporate holdings as related for the purposes of applying the trust surcharge if the company is a trustee. The mechanics vary by state and are frequently litigated; always model your specific fact pattern with a tax adviser before relying on threshold planning. Exemptions and principal place of residence carve-outs Every state exempts your principal place of residence from land tax, provided you (or a beneficiary, in the case of a trust) occupy it as your main home. The exemption applies to the land on which the dwelling sits, plus a curtilage allowance—typically up to 2 hectares, though the exact rules differ by state. Primary production land is exempt or concessionally rated in most jurisdictions, on the premise that farming is a business rather than passive investment. Victoria offers a primary production exemption if the land is used principally for farming and meets minimum income thresholds; NSW provides a similar exemption but requires that the land be used for the dominant purpose of a primary production business. If you lease farmland to a third-party operator, the exemption may not apply—check with your state revenue office before assuming the land is tax-free. Charitable and community organisations enjoy broad exemptions. Land owned by registered charities, religious institutions, public hospitals, and certain educational bodies is generally exempt, though the land must be used for the exempt purpose. If a charity leases part of its land to a commercial tenant, that portion becomes taxable. Boarding houses and build-to-rent schemes attract concessional treatment in some states. Victoria introduced a 50% land tax discount for new build-to-rent developments in 2022, provided the project meets minimum size, tenure and affordability criteria. NSW offers a similar concession under its build-to-rent land tax pathway, which applies a flat $30 per dwelling rate instead of the general ad valorem tax. These schemes are designed to encourage institutional investment in rental housing; individual investors generally do not qualify unless they partner with a registered community housing provider. Vacant land surcharges have been introduced in Victoria (vacant residential land tax) and parts of NSW and Queensland (local council vacancy charges). These sit on top of general land tax and penalise owners who leave homes or apartments unoccupied for more than six months. The Victorian vacant residential land tax is 1% of the capital improved value per year; it applies to residential property in designated inner and middle suburbs that is vacant for more than six months in the preceding calendar year. The surcharge is separate from land tax and uses a different valuation base (CIV vs site value), which can be confusing when calculating total holding costs. Stamp duty vs land tax trade-offs State governments have long debated replacing upfront stamp duty with a broader annual land tax. The ACT is the furthest along this path: since 2012 it has been phasing out conveyance duty and lifting general rates and land tax to compensate. By 2024 the ACT's annual land tax revenue rivals its diminishing stamp duty take, and new purchasers pay significantly lower upfront duty than in other states—but also face higher annual rates for the life of ownership. The cash-flow impact differs markedly. Stamp duty is a one-time impost—often 4% to 5.5% of the purchase price—that must be funded at settlement, usually from savings or by increasing the loan amount. Land tax is smaller in any given year (typically 0.5% to 2% of land value) but compounds over decades. For a $1 million purchase in NSW: Stamp duty (assuming non-FHOG, non-foreign): approximately $40,000 upfront Land tax (if land value is $600,000): $0 in year one (below threshold if this is your only holding), rising as you acquire more properties or land values increase If you hold the property for 30 years and land values grow 3% per annum, cumulative land tax could exceed the initial stamp duty bill, particularly once aggregation pushes you into higher brackets. Conversely, stamp duty is a sunk cost: you cannot recover it on sale (though it forms part of the CGT cost base), whereas ceasing to own land immediately stops the annual tax. Because land tax is deductible against rental income but stamp duty is not (it is capitalised), the after-tax cost differs. A trust paying $15,000 land tax and distributing income to a beneficiary on the 45% marginal rate effectively pays $8,250 after tax; the same trust that paid $40,000 stamp duty cannot deduct that amount and loses the full $40,000 in cash. Policymakers favour land tax because it is harder to avoid—you cannot move land offshore—and it discourages land banking. Investors often prefer stamp duty because it is finite and predictable. The tension between these views shapes every state budget; expect ongoing tinkering with thresholds, rates and surcharges as governments seek revenue without triggering voter backlash. What this means for your portfolio Land tax is the silent portfolio killer. It does not generate headlines like interest-rate rises or vacancy campaigns, yet a $20,000 annual bill over 25 years extracts $500,000 of cash that could have compounded into another property. The difference between a trust structure paying a 2% surcharge and an individual ownership model can be $40,000 per year—enough to fund the deposit on a fourth property within five years. If you are acquiring your first or second investment, consider whether your state's threshold gives you runway to grow within an individual structure before committing to a trust. Aggregation planning can save six figures over a portfolio's lifetime. Splitting ownership between spouses, using separate trusts with genuinely independent appointors, or parking high-value land in an SMSF (once your super balance allows) can each keep portions of your portfolio below the threshold or away from surcharges. The trade-off is complexity: multiple entities mean multiple tax returns, and lenders often treat trust borrowings more conservatively than personal loans. Annual review is non-negotiable.
