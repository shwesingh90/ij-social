Trust Distribution Resolution Deadline 30 June | InvestorJoint + Structure · 14 Nov 2026 · 8 min read Trust Distribution Resolutions: The 30 June Deadline Explained Miss the 30 June deadline for your trust distribution resolution and you'll pay tax at trustee rates—up to 47%. Here's why timing matters and what happens if you're late. If you hold investment property in a discretionary (family) trust, your trustee must pass a valid distribution resolution before midnight on 30 June each financial year. Miss that deadline and the ATO will tax undistributed income at the top marginal rate—currently 47%—or push it to a default beneficiary you never intended. For a trust with $60,000 of rental profit, that's the difference between paying $0 in tax (distributed to a low-income beneficiary) and $28,200 in trustee tax. The 30 June rule is absolute. Unlike a tax return lodgement—which you can extend—a distribution resolution is a legal act that must occur in the income year to which it relates. The ATO does not accept "we meant to" or "we signed it in July." This post explains why the deadline exists, the two penalties for missing it, and the practical steps trustees of property-owning trusts must take every year before 30 June. --- Why discretionary trusts need annual resolutions A discretionary trust does not have fixed entitlements. The trustee decides each year who receives income and capital, choosing from a pool of beneficiaries named in the trust deed. That flexibility is the reason property investors use family trusts: rental profit can be streamed to the spouse, adult child, or company with the lowest marginal tax rate, or retained to offset a capital loss. But flexibility requires a decision. Under Division 6 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, trust income is taxed to a beneficiary only if they are presently entitled to a share of that income by the end of the income year. Present entitlement arises when the trustee exercises discretion in favour of a beneficiary and that beneficiary has an immediate, indefeasible right to call for payment—even if cash is not actually paid. The act that creates present entitlement is the distribution resolution (sometimes called a distribution minute). It must be: Made by the trustee (or appointor, if the deed requires it) Recorded in writing Dated on or before 30 June of the relevant financial year Consistent with the powers in the trust deed If no valid resolution exists by 30 June, the ATO's position is that beneficiaries have not become presently entitled, and the income is either taxed to the trustee at penalty rates or defaults to a nominated beneficiary under the deed. --- The two penalties for missing the 30 June deadline 1. Trustee assessment at 47% If no beneficiary is presently entitled and the trust deed does not nominate a default beneficiary, the trustee is assessed on the undistributed income under section 99A ITAA 1936. The rate is the top marginal rate plus Medicare levy —47% for FY 2024–25. Example: A family trust owns two residential properties in Brisbane. Net rental income for FY 2024–25 is $55,000. The trustee intends to distribute the income to an adult daughter (taxable income $25,000, marginal rate 19%). The daughter's accountant prepares the resolution in early July and backdates it to 30 June. The ATO will disregard the resolution. The trust is assessed on $55,000 at 47% = $25,850 in tax . Had the resolution been signed on 30 June, the daughter would have paid approximately $5,700, and the family saves $20,150. Backdating is not merely discouraged—it is ineffective for tax purposes and may expose the trustee to penalties if the ATO considers it an attempt to mislead. 2. Unintended distribution to a default beneficiary Many trust deeds include a default beneficiary clause : if the trustee fails to resolve a distribution by 30 June, income is deemed to flow to a named person or entity. Common defaults are: The principal beneficiary (often the settlor or the person who established the trust) All beneficiaries in equal shares A nominated charity This can produce absurd outcomes. If the default beneficiary is the primary income earner on a $180,000 salary, the trust's $60,000 rental profit is added to their assessable income, taxed at 45% + Medicare (47% effective). If the default is a charity, the trustee may be obliged to pay the amount to the charity—removing it from the family permanently. State revenue offices may also reassess land tax. In New South Wales, trusts with a "fixed entitlement" default may lose the general land tax threshold ($1,075,000 for FY 2024–25) and be treated as a fixed trust with a lower threshold. --- Common mistakes that invalidate a resolution Even if signed before 30 June, a distribution resolution can be invalid if it breaches the trust deed or fails to meet legal requirements: 1. Wrong decision-maker. Some deeds require the appointor (not the trustee) to approve distributions above a certain amount. Others require written consent from a guardian or protector. 2. Distribution exceeds trust income. If the deed defines "income" using accounting profit and the resolution purports to distribute $80,000 when net profit is $60,000, the excess may be void. 3. Benefit to excluded persons. Most deeds exclude certain people (e.g., divorced spouses, non-relatives). Distributing to an excluded beneficiary is void. 4. Conditional or uncertain entitlement. A resolution that says "income to be split between A and B, details to be confirmed" does not create present entitlement because the amount is not ascertainable. 5. Unsigned or undated. A draft minute emailed on 29 June but signed on 2 July is late. The legal act is the signing, not the drafting. The ATO's ruling TR 2012/D1 (draft, but widely applied) confirms that a beneficiary must have a "vested and indefeasible interest in possession" by 30 June. Ambiguity, conditions, or trustee discretion to vary the distribution after 30 June will generally defeat present entitlement. --- Streaming rental income vs. capital gains Discretionary trusts that own property often have two types of income in a given year: rental income and capital gains (if a property is sold). Since 2011, the ATO permits trustees to stream these separately, provided the trust deed and resolution are specific. Example: A family trust in Victoria sells an investment property in March 2025, realising a $200,000 capital gain (50% discount applied = $100,000 taxable gain). The trust also earns $40,000 rental income from a remaining property. The trustee resolves before 30 June 2025 to: Distribute the $100,000 capital gain to the family company (which has $90,000 of carried-forward revenue losses) Distribute the $40,000 rental income to the non-working spouse (taxable income $12,000) The company pays $0 tax (gain offset by losses). The spouse pays approximately $5,320. Total tax: $5,320 . If the trustee had simply resolved "all income to the company," the $40,000 rental income would also flow to the company, wasting the spouse's tax-free threshold and low marginal brackets. Streaming requires the deed to define "income" by reference to tax categories (not just accounting profit) and the resolution to allocate each category separately. Many older deeds do not permit streaming; amending the deed requires trustee and (usually) appointor consent, and may trigger stamp duty in some states if property is held. For a detailed comparison of how trusts, companies, and personal ownership affect tax outcomes, see the trust vs. personal calculator . --- Practical steps before 30 June every year Trustees of property-holding discretionary trusts should follow this annual checklist: 1. Finalise estimated income by mid-June Work with your accountant to estimate: Rental income (use the rental cashflow calculator to reconcile rents received vs. expenses paid) Deductible expenses, including depreciation (the depreciation calculator can help if you do not have a quantity surveyor's report) Capital gains or losses from any property sales Franked dividends, interest, or other trust income You do not need final figures—estimates are sufficient for the resolution. Adjustments can be made when lodging the trust return. 2. Identify beneficiaries and their marginal rates List all eligible beneficiaries and their expected taxable income for the year. Consider: Low-income adult children (students, part-time workers) Non-working spouses Beneficiary companies with carried-forward losses Superannuation funds (if the deed permits and the fund is a listed beneficiary) For beneficiaries under 18, be mindful of Division 6AA: unearned income above $1,307 (FY 2024–25) is taxed at penalty rates up to 66%. Excepted income (e.g., income from a deceased estate) is treated differently; confirm the deed definitions. 3. Draft and sign the resolution before 30 June The resolution should: State the financial year Identify each beneficiary and the dollar amount or percentage of each income type Be signed by all trustees (or the appointor, if required) Be dated on or before 30 June If 30 June falls on a weekend, the deadline does not extend to the next business day. A resolution signed on 1 July is late. 4. Pay or record the entitlement in trust accounts Present entitlement does not require actual payment by 30 June, but the beneficiary must have a legal right to demand payment. The trust's accounts should record a liability (beneficiary loan account) equal to the distribution. Paying the distribution during the year (or soon after) avoids Division 7A issues if a beneficiary is a company or complications under section 100A if funds are lent back to the trust. 5. Keep evidence Retain: Signed and dated minutes Trustee meeting notes or appointor consents Correspondence with the accountant Bank statements showing payments to beneficiaries The ATO can request evidence of present entitlement during a review or audit. A printed email timestamp, signed PDF, or statutory declaration from the trustee may be required to prove the resolution was made before midnight on 30 June. --- Section 100A: the "reimbursement agreement" trap Even a timely, valid resolution can be challenged if the ATO applies section 100A ITAA 1936. This anti-avoidance rule targets arrangements where: A beneficiary is made presently entitled to trust income (and taxed on it at a low rate), but Another individual enjoys the economic benefit (e.g., the funds are lent back to a high-income family member or used to pay their private expenses) Example: A discretionary trust distributes $80,000 to an adult daughter (university student, no other income). The daughter is assessed on $80,000 and pays approximately $18,000 tax. The trustee then lends the $80,000 back to the father (a surgeon on $400,000 income) as an interest-free, unsecured loan. The ATO may apply section 100A to disregard the daughter's entitlement and assess the $80,000 to the trustee at 47%. Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2022/2 sets out safe harbours, but the core principle is that distributions should reflect genuine benefit. To avoid section 100A: Pay distributions to the beneficiary's own bank account Do not lend funds back to the trust or other family members at non-commercial terms Document any loans with written agreements, market interest, and repayment schedules Avoid circular arrangements (beneficiary pays school fees; trust reimburses beneficiary) This is a live compliance focus. The ATO issued over 100 section 100A notices in FY 2022–23, many involving family trusts with rental properties. --- What this means for your portfolio If you own property through a discretionary trust, the 30 June distribution deadline is the single most important date in your tax calendar.
