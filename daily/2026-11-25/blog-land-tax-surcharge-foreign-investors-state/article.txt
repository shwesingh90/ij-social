Land Tax Surcharge Foreign Investors by State | InvestorJoint + States · 24 Nov 2026 · 8 min read Foreign Investor Land Tax Surcharge by State (2026) Foreign and absentee owners face land tax surcharges up to 4% in some states. Here's what you'll pay in NSW, Victoria, Queensland, and SA—and how holding structures affect the bill. The Bottom Line: What Foreign and Absentee Investors Pay If you're a foreign investor or an Australian citizen living overseas, you'll face a land tax surcharge in every mainland state—even if you hold your property in a trust or company. NSW charges foreign owners an additional 4% flat surcharge on top of standard land tax, Victoria applies 4% on the unimproved capital value, Queensland levies 2% above the standard rate, and South Australia imposes 0.5%. These surcharges stack on top of regular land tax, kick in at the same (or lower) thresholds, and cannot be avoided simply by changing the ownership entity—RevenueSA, the State Revenue Office Victoria, and Revenue NSW all look through structures to determine beneficial ownership and residency status. Who Counts as "Foreign" or "Absentee" The definition varies slightly by state, but the core test is consistent: foreign persons are individuals who are not Australian citizens or permanent residents, plus corporations and trusts in which foreign persons hold a controlling interest (typically ≥50% by value or voting power). An absentee owner is an Australian citizen or permanent resident who does not ordinarily reside in Australia—usually defined as being out of the country for more than six months in the relevant calendar or financial year. Victoria, NSW, and Queensland all use the term "absentee" to capture expat Australians. South Australia historically used "foreign" but expanded the net to include certain absentee arrangements. Trusts and companies are tested on the residency and citizenship of their beneficiaries, unitholders, or shareholders. A discretionary family trust with an Australian-resident individual trustee will still be classified as foreign if foreign persons can benefit or if a foreign person controls the trustee. If you're unsure whether your structure triggers the surcharge, check the beneficial ownership at 31 December (Victoria, SA, WA) or 30 June (NSW, Queensland). Most revenue offices require an annual declaration; failing to lodge or lodging late can result in an automatic foreign-person classification and penalty interest. New South Wales: 4% Flat Surcharge NSW imposes a 4% surcharge on the taxable value of land held by foreign persons, applied to the entire landholding that exceeds the tax-free threshold. For the 2025–26 land tax year (assessed on landholdings as at 31 December 2025), the general threshold is $1,075,000 for individuals and special trusts, and $6,571,000 for companies and standard trusts. The 4% foreign-person surcharge applies in addition to the progressive land tax scale. For example, if a Singapore-based investor owns an investment property in Sydney with a land value of $1.5 million: Standard land tax: $6,650 (base) + 1.6% of value above $1,075,000 = $6,650 + $6,800 = $13,450 Foreign surcharge: 4% × $1,500,000 = $60,000 Total land tax: $73,450 By comparison, an Australian resident with identical landholdings would pay only $13,450. The surcharge does not apply to a taxpayer's principal place of residence if they are an Australian citizen or permanent resident living in that home—but foreign persons receive no principal-place-of-residence exemption even if they occupy the property. Revenue NSW publishes an annual land tax guide each January and updates foreign-person rates in line with Budget announcements. Victoria: 4% Absentee Owner Surcharge Victoria's absentee owner surcharge is 4% of the site value (unimproved capital value) of all land held by an absentee owner as at 31 December each year. The surcharge is levied on landholdings above the general threshold of $50,000 for trusts (from 2024) and $300,000 for individuals and other entities. The Victorian rules treat the surcharge as an additional tier: once your total Victorian land value exceeds the threshold, you pay the standard progressive land tax plus 4% on the entire taxable value. For example, an Australian expat living in London who owns two investment units in Melbourne with combined site values of $800,000 will pay: Standard land tax (using the 2025 rates): approximately $4,975 Absentee surcharge: 4% × $800,000 = $32,000 Total: $36,975 A resident owner with the same landholding pays only the $4,975 standard component. The State Revenue Office Victoria requires an annual absentee-owner declaration by 15 January. If you fail to lodge, the SRO will presume you are an absentee and apply the 4% surcharge automatically, plus penalty interest at the higher rate (currently around 10% p.a. compounding daily). Victoria also offers a principal-place-of-residence exemption that wholly exempts land from land tax—but only if you are not an absentee. If you are an Australian citizen living overseas and retain a Melbourne apartment as your "main residence," the land remains taxable and subject to the surcharge unless you return to ordinary residency in Australia. Queensland: 2% Absentee Surcharge Queensland's foreign-owner regime is known as the absentee surcharge , levied at 2% on the freehold land component of property owned by absentees and foreign individuals or corporations. The 2025–26 thresholds are $600,000 for individuals and $350,000 for companies and trusts. The 2% surcharge is added to the standard progressive scale. For instance, a New Zealand citizen (who does not hold Australian permanent residency) owning an investment property in Brisbane with a land value of $750,000 pays: Standard land tax: approximately $1,500 (using the individual scale above $600k) Absentee surcharge: 2% × $750,000 = $15,000 Total: $16,500 Residents pay only the $1,500 standard component. Queensland Revenue Office assesses landholdings as at 30 June each year, and absentee status is determined for the full financial year preceding the assessment. If you spent fewer than 183 days in Australia during FY 2024–25, you are deemed an absentee for the 2025–26 land tax year. Foreign companies and trusts with any foreign ownership interest above 50% automatically trigger the 2% surcharge, regardless of whether individual shareholders or beneficiaries are absentees. Discretionary trusts are particularly scrutinised: if the trustee can distribute to a foreign person, the trust is classified as foreign unless you obtain a private ruling confirming that no foreign person benefits. The absentee surcharge does not apply to land subject to primary production exemptions, so rural holdings may escape the extra 2% if they qualify. For urban investment properties, factor the surcharge into your rental cash-flow calculator to understand true net yield after all state levies. South Australia: 0.5% Foreign-Owner Surcharge South Australia introduced a 0.5% surcharge on land tax for foreign owners from the 2019–20 land tax year. The surcharge applies to land valued above the general threshold of $626,000 for the 2025–26 year (indexed annually). Foreign natural persons, foreign corporations, and trusts with foreign beneficiaries or controllers all fall within scope. For example, a Singaporean investor holding an Adelaide investment property with a site value of $900,000 will pay: Standard land tax: approximately $6,170 (using the 2025 scale) Foreign surcharge: 0.5% × $900,000 = $4,500 Total: $10,670 A resident owner pays $6,170. While SA's 0.5% surcharge is the lowest in the country, it still represents a material ongoing cost—especially when combined with the state's 7% foreign-buyer stamp-duty surcharge on acquisition. RevenueSA assesses land tax on landholdings at 30 June each year and requires a foreign-ownership declaration if you hold land through a trust or company. South Australia does not use the term "absentee"; the surcharge applies strictly to foreign persons under the Land Tax Act 1936 (SA). Australian citizens living overseas are not subject to the surcharge unless they hold property through a foreign-controlled entity. This makes SA relatively attractive for expat Australians compared to Victoria or NSW, where absentee status alone triggers the higher 4% rate. Western Australia, Tasmania, ACT, and NT Western Australia does not levy a separate land tax surcharge for foreign or absentee owners. Standard land tax applies equally to all, though WA imposes a 7% transfer duty surcharge on foreign buyers at acquisition. Absentee owners pay the same annual land tax as residents—a significant advantage if you're an expat Australian holding Perth property. Tasmania similarly has no foreign or absentee land tax surcharge, though it introduced a 3% foreign-buyer duty surcharge in 2018. The land tax threshold for 2025–26 is $50,000 (trust) or $100,000 (individual), and rates are progressive regardless of residency. The Australian Capital Territory abolished land tax surcharges for foreign and absentee owners in 2023 as part of its shift to a broad-based land tax (which applies to all landholdings including owner-occupied homes). The ACT's progressive land tax scale now applies uniformly, with foreign buyers paying an additional 1% on the standard conveyance duty rate. The Northern Territory does not impose land tax at all, making it unique among Australian jurisdictions. Foreign and absentee investors face a 1% duty surcharge on acquisition but no ongoing annual land tax burden. For investors considering multi-state portfolios, WA and Tasmania offer meaningful savings on recurring costs if absentee or foreign status applies, while the NT's zero land tax makes it compelling for land-banking strategies despite smaller rental markets. How Trusts and Companies Are Tested Every state revenue office looks through the legal ownership entity to identify the beneficial owners . A discretionary family trust does not shield you from the foreign or absentee surcharge if: A foreign person is named as a beneficiary in the trust deed, or A foreign person can be added as a beneficiary by amendment or by virtue of a broad definition class (e.g., "any person the trustee nominates"), or The appointor or controlling trustee is a foreign person or absentee. Companies are tested by shareholding : if ≥50% of shares (by value or voting rights) are held by foreign persons or absentees, the company is classified as foreign. Unit trusts apply the same 50% test to unitholders. Even minority foreign ownership can taint a structure in some states if the foreign unitholder also exercises control over the trustee or holds a casting vote. Self-managed super funds (SMSFs) can be caught if a member is an absentee or foreign person. For example, an SMSF with two members—one Australian resident and one living in Dubai—will be deemed an absentee owner in Victoria and NSW, triggering the 4% surcharge on the fund's entire landholding. RevenueSA and the SRO Victoria both explicitly state that the SMSF is not exempt from the surcharge simply because it is a complying super fund. Practical Strategies to Manage Surcharge Exposure 1. Maintain Australian residency : If you are an Australian citizen, ensure you spend at least 183 days per year in Australia to retain resident status for land tax purposes. Keep records of travel, lease agreements, and utilities to prove ordinary residency if the revenue office queries your declaration. 2. Restructure before you go : If an overseas posting is temporary, consider transferring investment property into the name of a resident spouse or adult child (subject to CGT, stamp duty, and mortgage consent). This can avoid the 4% surcharge for the period of absence. 3. Quarantine foreign ownership in separate trusts : If a family trust has both resident and non-resident beneficiaries, establish a separate trust for the foreign beneficiary's entitlements. This prevents the entire trust from being tainted and allows the Australian-resident trust to hold property without surcharge. 4. Review discretionary trust deeds : If your deed includes broad "add or remove beneficiaries" powers, consider narrowing the class to exclude foreign persons explicitly. Obtain a private ruling from the relevant state revenue office confirming the trust is not foreign before lodging your next land tax return. 5. Model the total cost of offshore ownership : The surcharge is only one component. Factor in foreign-buyer stamp duty (7–8%), higher mortgage rates (lenders often apply LVR caps and rate loadings for non-residents), CGT withholding (12.5% on sale proceeds), and reduced negative gearing benefit (if you have no Australian-sourced income). InvestorJoint's negative gearing calculator and borrowing capacity calculator help quantify the true after-tax return. 6. Use depreciation to offset the surcharge : While you cannot reduce the land tax liability itself, maximising depreciation deductions improves your overall cash flow. Run a depreciation calculator report to identify all capital works and plant-and-equipment write-offs, especially if the property was built or renovated after 1985.
