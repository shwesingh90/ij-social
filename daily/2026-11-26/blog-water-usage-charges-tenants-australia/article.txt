Water Usage Charges Tenants Australia | InvestorJoint + Structure · 25 Nov 2026 · 8 min read Water Usage Charges: When Tenants Pay and When You Do In most Australian states, landlords can pass water usage charges to tenants—but only if the property meets water-efficiency standards. Here's the state-by-state breakdown. In most Australian states, landlords can recover water usage charges from tenants—but not the fixed supply charge—and only if the property meets specific water-efficiency standards. Get it wrong and you'll absorb a cost that can run to $800–1,200 per year for a typical three-bedroom house, or face a tribunal claim if you've over-recovered. Water billing splits into two components everywhere in Australia: a fixed supply charge (sometimes called the service or access charge) and a variable usage charge based on kilolitres consumed. The supply charge pays for the infrastructure; usage pays for the water itself. The national pattern is clear—landlords almost always wear the supply charge, and tenants may pay usage if certain conditions are met—but the detail varies by state and can trip up multi-property portfolios that span jurisdictions. National framework: supply vs usage Every state and territory residential tenancy act draws the same bright line: Supply charge : always the landlord's cost. This covers meter reading, billing, infrastructure maintenance, and network access. In FY 2024 it ranges from around $220 per annum in Adelaide to over $300 in Sydney, depending on the retailer and meter size. Usage charge : recoverable from the tenant only if the lease permits it and statutory preconditions are satisfied. The logic is straightforward: the landlord owns the asset and benefits from capital growth, so fixed costs of ownership stay with them. Usage is the tenant's day-to-day consumption, so it's fair game—but only when the landlord has taken reasonable steps to prevent waste. If you operate through a family trust or company structure, the entity that holds legal title is the "landlord" for billing purposes. Water retailers will issue the account in the entity name, and you'll need to on-charge usage to the tenant under the lease. That adds an extra reconciliation step each quarter, which is why platforms like InvestorJoint track water invoices at the entity level and automate tenant recovery journals. NSW: individually metered and water-efficient In New South Wales, section 39 of the Residential Tenancies Act 2010 lets landlords recover water usage charges if three conditions are met: 1. The property is individually metered (not on a strata master meter shared with other lots). 2. The property is water-efficient : all internal cold-water taps and single-mixer taps have a maximum flow rate of 9 litres per minute, and all toilets have a dual-flush capacity of no more than 3/6 litres per flush. 3. The landlord provides the tenant with a copy of the water bill within three months of the bill date. The 9 L/min tap-flow rule and 3/6 L dual-flush standard have been in place since March 2008, so almost every post-2008 property complies by default. Older properties may need retrofits: tap aerators cost $5–15 each at Bunnings, and a dual-flush conversion kit is $30–60. If you fail to comply, the tenant can dispute the charge at the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal, and you'll wear the cost—potentially $400–600 per quarter for a family home. One trap: strata properties with embedded networks or bulk hot-water systems cannot recover usage unless the tenant has a dedicated meter reading their actual consumption. If the owners corporation bills you a share of the building's total consumption, that's your cost to absorb. Victoria: all usage recoverable by default—if water-efficient Victorian landlords have the simplest regime. Under section 44 of the Residential Tenancies Act 1997 , a tenant must pay "the full amount payable for water usage" if the property meets water-efficiency standards. There is no requirement to include a special term in the lease—it's the statutory default. The Victorian water-efficiency checklist (prescribed in the Residential Tenancies Regulations 2021 ) requires: All internal cold-water taps and single-mixer taps: maximum 9 L/min flow rate. All showerheads: maximum 9 L/min flow rate. All toilets: dual flush, maximum 3/6 litres or single flush 4.5 litres. You must provide the tenant with a copy of the water bill within 60 days. If you miss the deadline, the tenant can apply to Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (VCAT) to have the charge waived. One quirk: if the property is on tank water or bore water, usage is not separately metered by a retailer, so there's no "bill" to pass on—tank refills and pump electricity stay with the landlord. Queensland: metered, efficient, and lease-specified Queensland's rules sit under section 161 and Schedule 1 of the Residential Tenancies and Rooming Accommodation Act 2008 . A landlord can recover water usage only if: 1. The property has an individual meter that measures water supplied only to the premises (not shared). 2. The property is water-efficient to the same 9 L/min taps, 9 L/min shower, and dual-flush 3/6 L or single 4.5 L toilet standard. 3. The lease or a separate written agreement states the tenant must pay for water usage. Unlike Victoria, Queensland requires you to draft the lease term explicitly. A standard REIQ lease will have a tick-box; if you self-manage, include wording such as "The tenant must pay for all water usage charges as permitted by the Act, subject to compliance with water-efficiency standards." Landlords must give the tenant a copy of the water bill or invoice within one month of receiving it. If the retailer bills quarterly, that quarter's usage becomes recoverable only after you've handed over the invoice—so timely admin matters. One edge case: rural properties on scheme water often have higher fixed charges and tiered usage rates. The same usage-only rule applies, but check whether your local council or SunWater bill separately itemises supply versus usage. Some older rural invoices lump them together, which can cause disputes. South Australia, WA, Tasmania, ACT, and NT: quick summary South Australia : Section 71 of the Residential Tenancies Act 1995 allows full recovery of water usage if the property is separately metered and water-efficient (same 9 L/min and dual-flush standards). No special lease clause needed; it's statutory. Landlord must provide the bill "as soon as practicable." Western Australia : Section 43 of the Residential Tenancies Act 1987 permits recovery of usage charges only if the lease says so and the property is separately metered. Water-efficiency requirements do not exist in WA legislation—yet. In practice, most metro leases include a water-usage clause, but regional properties on scheme water sometimes omit it. Tasmania : Section 35A of the Residential Tenancy Act 1997 allows usage recovery if the property is individually metered and meets water-efficiency standards (9 L/min taps and showers, dual flush 4.5/3 L). Landlord must provide the bill within four weeks and can charge only the amount billed by the water authority—no margin. Australian Capital Territory : Section 23 of the Residential Tenancies Act 1997 (ACT) says a tenant pays water usage if the lease requires it and the property is separately metered. Water-efficiency standards are not mandated by tenancy law, but Icon Water's billing structure makes this a minor point—usage charges are low, and landlords rarely bother disputing compliance. Northern Territory : Section 51 of the Residential Tenancies Act 1999 (NT) requires the lease to specify that the tenant pays usage. The property must be individually metered. Water-efficiency standards are not prescribed. Darwin and Palmerston usage charges are modest—typically $150–250 per annum for a house—so many landlords include usage recovery as a lease term but don't retrofit taps. Water-efficiency compliance: retrofits and certification If your portfolio includes properties built before 2008, audit each one before passing usage charges to new tenants. A non-compliant tap or toilet is a $5–60 fix at the hardware store, but failing to retrofit can cost you $1,200 per year per property in unrecovered usage. Tap aerators : screw onto the spout and restrict flow. Look for the WELS (Water Efficiency Labelling and Standards) star rating—3-star aerators deliver around 6–7.5 L/min. Showerheads : must display a WELS label showing maximum flow. A 3-star head is typically 6–7 L/min; older "rain" showers can exceed 15 L/min. Toilets : single-flush models over 6 litres are non-compliant. Dual-flush retrofit kits replace the cistern valve and buttons; a plumber can install one in 20 minutes for around $80 labour. Record-keeping : take date-stamped photos of WELS labels and keep hardware-store receipts. If a tenant disputes compliance at tribunal, you'll need proof. Some landlords attach a water-efficiency checklist to the ingoing condition report signed by the tenant. One practical tip: after a tenancy ends, check that replacement taps or showerheads installed by the outgoing tenant still meet the standard. Tenants sometimes swap in a high-flow "massage" showerhead and forget to reinstate the original; if the next tenant complains and the head is 12 L/min, you'll be on the back foot. Invoicing, timing, and payment arrangements State rules set deadlines for providing the water bill to your tenant, but they don't prescribe how the tenant must pay. Common approaches: 1. Pay-on-receipt : tenant reimburses the landlord or property manager within 14–21 days of receiving a copy of the bill. This is cleanest for cash flow but requires the landlord to pay the retailer upfront. 2. Periodic deduction : increase rent by an estimate of quarterly usage (say $150) and reconcile annually. This smooths cash flow but can cause disputes if actual usage is higher or lower. 3. Direct billing : some retailers (SA Water, for example) offer a "tenant billing" service where the tenant's name goes on the usage portion of the account. The landlord still receives the supply charge separately. Few investors use this because it adds admin and tenants may fall behind, leaving the landlord liable. Whatever method you choose, record the transaction correctly in your trust or company accounts. If you're recovering $600 per annum in water usage, that's assessable income to the landlord entity (even though it offsets an equivalent expense). Your tax pack should show water expense gross and water recovery as other income, not net the two, so your accountant can trace cost recovery if the ATO queries rental income. What this means for your portfolio Water-usage recovery is one of those line items that looks small on a per-property basis but compounds across a portfolio of five or ten properties—$5,000–10,000 per year in aggregate. It also exposes compliance risk: if even one property in your trust fails the tap-flow test and a tenant challenges it, that tribunal decision can flow through to other tenancies when word spreads on tenant forums.
