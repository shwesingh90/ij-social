Selling an Investment Property Tax Checklist | InvestorJoint + Tax · 27 Nov 2026 · 9 min read Selling an Investment Property: A Tax Checklist Before You Sign Capital gains tax can wipe out 23.5–47% of your profit if you skip the groundwork. This checklist covers cost base, depreciation clawback, and timing traps before you sign the contract. The difference between signing a contract and walking away with the cheque can be six months—and tens of thousands of dollars in tax. For Australian property investors, capital gains tax (CGT) is triggered on the contract date , not settlement, and you'll pay anywhere from 23.5% to 47% of your gain unless you've built a defensible cost base, structured ownership correctly, and accounted for every depreciation dollar you've claimed. This checklist walks you through the tax mechanics before you list, so you're not scrambling in June to reconstruct five years of renovations. Understand when CGT is triggered: contract vs settlement The Australian Taxation Office is clear: a CGT event happens on the date you enter into the contract to sell, not the day funds hit your account. If you sign on 25 June 2025 and settle on 15 August 2025, you must report the capital gain in your FY2024–25 tax return—even though the cash arrives in FY2025–26. This timing quirk matters for three reasons. First, if you're close to the 30 June threshold and your marginal rate is about to drop (say, you're retiring or taking parental leave), delaying the contract by a week can shift the entire gain into a lower-income year. Second, if you're holding in a discretionary trust, the contract date locks in which beneficiaries can receive distributions of the capital gain; you cannot retrospectively change trust distributions once 30 June passes. Third, pre-CGT assets (acquired before 20 September 1985) remain exempt only if the original contract was signed before that date—so double-check your records if you inherited or bought an older property. State stamp duty and settlement adjustments (rates, water, body corporate) are separate: they crystallise at settlement. But for CGT purposes, contract date is king. Calculate your cost base—everything you're entitled to add Your taxable capital gain is sale price minus cost base. The larger the cost base, the smaller the tax bill. Division 110 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 lets you include five categories of expenditure, and many investors leave money on the table by forgetting the small stuff. Purchase costs include the original price, stamp duty (check your state revenue office receipt), conveyancing and solicitor fees, building and pest inspection reports, and mortgage establishment or discharge fees tied to acquisition. If you bought via auction, the buyer's premium counts. If you used a buyer's agent, their fee is in. Capital improvements are works that increase the property's value or materially change its character. A new kitchen, bathroom renovation, granny flat, pergola, driveway extension, ducted air conditioning, or solar panel system all qualify. Repairs and maintenance do not —repainting in the same colour, fixing a leaking tap, or replacing broken tiles are deductible in the year incurred but don't add to cost base. The line is fuzzy: replacing a worn carpet with identical carpet is repair; upgrading to engineered timber is arguably improvement. Keep quotes, invoices, and proof of payment (bank statements) for every job over \$300. Ownership costs cover rates, land tax, body corporate fees, and insurance premiums—but only for periods when the property was not rented or available for rent and did not produce assessable income. If you bought in January 2020, lived in it until June 2020, then rented it out, you can add six months of holding costs to the cost base. Once it's income-producing, those same expenses become annual deductions instead. Interest and borrowing costs follow the same rule: include them in the cost base only if the property is not yet income-producing. If you took out a \$600,000 loan and paid \$18,000 interest in the first year while renovating before tenants moved in, that \$18,000 (plus loan establishment fees, valuation, and lender's mortgage insurance) lifts your cost base. After the first tenant signs, interest becomes a yearly deduction. Sale costs are always added: agent commission (typically 1.5–2.5% plus GST), solicitor or conveyancer fees, marketing (photography, styling, online portals), and auctioneer fees. If you obtained a clearance certificate for a foreign-resident vendor or paid for a surveyor's certificate to resolve a boundary dispute before listing, include those too. A common mistake: failing to index the cost base for inflation. Australia abolished CGT indexation for assets acquired after 21 September 1999, replacing it with the 50% CGT discount. If you bought before that date and have held for more than twelve months, you can choose indexation or the discount—whichever gives the lower tax. For post-1999 purchases, discount only. Apply the CGT discount—and know who gets it Hold the property for at least twelve months from purchase contract to sale contract, and Australian resident individuals, trusts, and super funds can discount the capital gain. Individuals and trusts cut the gain by 50%; complying super funds (including SMSFs) get a 33⅓% discount. Companies receive no discount, which is why buying investment property in a company structure is rare outside commercial holdings. The twelve-month clock starts the day after you sign the purchase contract and ends on the sale contract date. If you bought on 10 March 2023 and sell on 10 March 2024, you're one day short—no discount. If you sell on 11 March 2024, you qualify. Trusts can distribute a discounted capital gain, but only the trust gets the discount first; beneficiaries do not apply a second 50% cut. If a family trust realises a \$200,000 gain, applies the 50% discount to arrive at \$100,000 taxable gain, and distributes that \$100,000 to an individual beneficiary on a 39% marginal rate (including Medicare levy), the tax is \$39,000. The beneficiary does not halve it again. SMSF trustees should note that the discount drops to zero once the fund enters pension phase on a given asset, because pension-phase earnings are tax-free anyway. If you're over 60, in retirement, and your SMSF property is supporting an account-based pension, the entire capital gain may be exempt—no discount needed. Confirm the asset's status in your fund's accounting records before you list. For more on SMSF property rules, see InvestorJoint's SMSF guide . Account for depreciation balancing adjustments Every dollar of building write-off (capital works deductions under Division 43) and plant-and-equipment depreciation (Division 40) you've claimed since 2017–18 will reduce your cost base on sale—or create a balancing adjustment that adds to your taxable income. Capital works (2.5% per year on the construction cost of post-1987 buildings) are straightforward: total claimed deductions come off your cost base. If you bought for \$650,000 (land \$250,000, building \$400,000) and claimed \$10,000 per year for five years (\$50,000 total), your building cost base drops to \$350,000. The land component is unchanged. Plant and equipment is trickier. Items like ovens, dishwashers, blinds, ceiling fans, and hot-water systems have a written-down value that must be reconciled on sale. If your depreciation schedule showed a \$2,000 oven with a \$200 written-down value at sale and you've claimed \$1,800 in deductions, you compare written-down value to sale proceeds for that item . Because fixtures are sold with the property, the ATO deems their sale proceeds to equal written-down value unless you can prove otherwise—meaning no immediate balancing adjustment. However, if you've over-claimed or the item was scrapped and replaced, a taxable balancing charge can arise. The May 2017 budget removed plant-and-equipment deductions for second-hand assets in residential property, so if you bought after 9 May 2017, you can only claim items you purchased new and installed yourself (e.g., a new split-system). Check whether your original depreciation schedule complies; some investors unknowingly claimed second-hand items and face amendment risk. Grab your depreciation schedules for every year of ownership and your accountant's tax returns. Add up capital works claimed, verify plant items, and feed the totals into your cost-base spreadsheet. If you've lost the schedule, contact the original quantity surveyor or use settlement and renovation records to reconstruct it. The ATO's data-matching picks up rental deductions, so under-reporting will trigger queries. Check if the main residence exemption applies (partially or fully) If you lived in the property as your principal place of residence before converting it to an investment, you may be entitled to a full or partial main residence exemption that zeroes out CGT for that period. Full exemption: If you bought on 1 July 2018, lived in it until 30 June 2020 (two years), then rented it for three years and sold on 30 June 2023 (five years total ownership), you can exempt the first two years plus up to six years of absence while it was rented—meaning the entire five-year holding qualifies. The "six-year rule" lets you treat the property as your main residence for up to six years after moving out, provided you don't claim another property as your main residence in that time and the dwelling was your main residence before you moved out. Partial exemption: If you rented it for eight years after living in it for two, you exempt two years under actual occupancy and six years under the absence rule, paying CGT on the remaining two years pro-rata. The exemption is not available if you first leased the property before moving in (e.g., bought off-the-plan in 2019, tenanted until 2021, then moved in). The ATO requires you to move in "as soon as practicable" after settlement to start the main residence clock. Document every move with tenancy agreements, electoral roll records, utility connections, and Medicare address changes. If you claimed the main residence exemption on another property in overlapping years, you'll need to apportion or choose which property gets the benefit for that period. Review ownership structure and distribution options Who holds title determines who pays the tax—and how much flexibility you have. Individuals and joint tenants: Gains are split according to legal ownership percentage. Two names as joint tenants means 50/50, even if one partner earned all the income to service the loan. Tenants in common can hold unequal shares (e.g., 99/1), and each reports their fraction of the gain. Discretionary trusts: The trustee can distribute the net capital gain to any eligible beneficiary in the same financial year, letting you allocate income to the family member with the lowest marginal rate. If your trust deed permits, you might distribute the \$100,000 discounted gain to an adult child on a 21% rate rather than the primary income earner on 47%. Distributions must be made by 30 June (or the trust's year-end) and documented in writing. Streaming rules (Subdivision 115-C) let you separately stream capital gains and franked dividends, so verify your deed allows it. Companies: The gain is taxed at 25% (base rate entity) or 30% (otherwise), with no discount. Profit is trapped until franked dividends are paid, triggering a second layer of tax in shareholders' hands. Companies make sense for active trading or development but rarely for buy-and-hold residential. SMSFs: Taxed at 15% on the discounted gain (10% effective rate) in accumulation, or 0% in pension phase if supporting a retirement income stream. Contributions and benefits rules apply; confirm the sale doesn't breach in-house asset or sole-purpose tests. Time the sale to manage your marginal rate and Medicare levy surcharge Because CGT is additional assessable income in the year of contract, a large gain can push you into a higher bracket or trigger the 1–1.5% Medicare levy surcharge if you don't have private hospital cover. For FY2024–25, the 37% bracket starts at \$135,001 and the 45% bracket at \$190,001 (including Medicare levy of 2%). If your salary is \$120,000 and you realise a \$150,000 discounted gain (\$75,000 taxable), your total assessable income jumps to \$195,000—lifting the top slice into the 47% bracket and costing roughly \$35,250 in CGT plus loss of offsets. Strategies to soften the blow: 1. Defer contract signing past 30 June if next year's income will be lower (redundancy, parental leave, retirement). 2. Bring forward deductions into the sale year—prepay interest (if your lender allows twelve months in advance), make concessional super contributions up to the \$30,000 cap, or accelerate repairs on other properties. 3. Stage multiple sales across financial years if you're offloading a portfolio; spreading three sales over three years keeps you in a lower bracket each time. 4. Use carry-forward capital losses from shares, managed funds, or prior property sales. Capital losses can only offset capital gains (not salary), so if you sold shares at a \$20,000 loss in FY2022–23 and carried the loss forward, you can apply it now to reduce the property gain. If you're on the borderline for Family Tax Benefit, Commonwealth Seniors Health Card, or the low-and-middle-income tax offset (now ceased but illustrative of means-tested benefits), model the effective marginal rate including benefit withdrawal. Sometimes a \$10,000 gain costs \$15,000 in combined tax and lost entitlements. Gather and organise your records now The ATO expects you to substantiate every cost-base claim. For a property held ten years, that means a decade of paperwork. Create a folder—digital or physical—with: Purchase contract and settlement statement (stamp duty, legals, agent fees). Loan documents: establishment fees, mortgage insurance, discharge fees. Depreciation schedules and quantity surveyor invoices. Renovation invoices and receipts: itemised, showing GST if you claimed input credits. Body corporate budgets and levy notices (to separate repair levies from capital improvement levies). Insurance renewals, rates notices, land tax assessments for non-income periods. Tenancy agreements and rent rolls to prove income-producing start and end dates. Agent statements showing management fees, letting fees, advertising. Sale contract, marketing agreement, and settlement statement. If you've used multiple accountants or changed software, reconcile claimed deductions year by year. The ATO's pre-fill data covers rental income and some bank interest but not cash payments to tradespeople or minor capital works. Scan everything and store it in a secure cloud folder. What this means for your portfolio Selling isn't just about the sale price—it's about knowing your after-tax proceeds before you commit. A \$900,000 contract might net you \$650,000 after agent fees, legals, and a \$160,000 CGT bill, or \$720,000 if you've kept every invoice and maximised cost base. That \$70,000 difference funds the next deposit or pays down non-deductible debt.
