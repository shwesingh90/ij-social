Rental Property Loan Interest Deductibility | InvestorJoint + Tax · 15 Nov 2026 · 8 min read When Is Rental Property Loan Interest Deductible? Interest on a rental property loan is deductible when the borrowed funds are used to earn assessable income. Once you redraw for private purposes, the deduction shrinks—and the ATO expects watertight records. Interest on a rental property loan is deductible against your assessable income only to the extent the borrowed funds are used to produce assessable income . The moment you redraw part of the loan to buy a car, renovate your family home or take a holiday, that portion of the interest loses its tax deduction—permanently. The ATO applies a strict purpose test to every dollar of principal, and if you can't prove a dollar was used to earn rent or other assessable income, the corresponding interest is not deductible. Mixing purposes inside a single loan facility creates a compliance headache that lasts the life of the loan. Below we unpack the purpose test, explain how redraws and offsets differ, show what happens when one loan funds multiple assets, and set out the record-keeping standard the ATO expects when it asks for evidence. --- The Purpose Test: Why You Borrowed, Not What You Secured Deductibility turns on use , not security. Section 8-1 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 allows a deduction for losses or outgoings "to the extent" they are incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. Taxation Ruling TR 2023/2 (which updated the long-standing TR 2000/2) clarifies that interest is deductible when the borrowed money is used for an income-producing purpose, regardless of what asset secures the loan. Practical example: You borrow $600,000 secured against your principal place of residence and use every cent to buy a rental property in Brisbane. The interest is fully deductible because the purpose is income production. Conversely, if you borrow $600,000 secured against an investment property but spend the funds renovating your own kitchen, zero interest is deductible—the purpose was private. This trips up investors who assume "investment property loan = deductible interest." The ATO will trace funds to their end use. If the bank statement shows a $50,000 redraw that paid for a caravan, the interest on that $50,000 slice is private, even though the loan remains secured by a rental property. --- Redraws That Kill Deductibility Many lenders offer redraw facilities on investment loans, letting you withdraw any extra principal you've paid down. Redraws are convenient but dangerous for tax. What happens when you redraw: 1. The outstanding principal increases by the redraw amount. 2. Interest now accrues on a larger balance. 3. You must apportion interest between the original income-producing purpose and the new private purpose. Example: Your investment loan starts at $500,000. You make extra repayments and reduce it to $450,000. You then redraw $30,000 to pay for a European holiday. Outstanding balance: $480,000. Income-producing portion: $450,000 (the amount still funding the rental). Private portion: $30,000. Deductible interest: only the portion attributable to $450,000. You must split every interest charge going forward. If the annual interest bill is $28,800, only (450,000 ÷ 480,000) × $28,800 = $27,000 is deductible; the remaining $1,800 is private. That apportionment continues until the loan is fully repaid . Future extra repayments do not "reset" the split; the ATO expects you to track which dollars relate to which purpose across the loan's life. --- Offset Accounts Preserve Simplicity An offset account is a separate transaction account whose balance reduces the interest charged on the linked loan, but the loan principal itself never changes. Because you never redraw from the loan, the purpose remains untainted. Example: Same $500,000 investment loan. You park $30,000 in a 100 % offset account. Interest is calculated on $470,000, and the entire interest charge remains deductible because the loan principal is still $500,000 used for the rental property. When you spend the $30,000 on a holiday, you withdraw it from the offset—not the loan—so no apportionment is required. Why this matters: Offset accounts let you access surplus cash without creating a mixed-purpose loan. InvestorJoint's negative gearing calculator assumes loan interest is fully deductible; if you've been redrawing for private use, your actual deduction—and net tax benefit—will be lower than the calculator suggests unless you manually adjust the interest figure. --- Mixed-Purpose Loans and Multi-Asset Scenarios Investors often use a single credit facility to fund several purchases or refinance existing debts. The ATO requires you to apportion interest by tracing each dollar of principal to its purpose . One Loan, Multiple Investment Properties If you borrow $800,000 and use $500,000 for a Sydney apartment and $300,000 for a Melbourne townhouse, the entire $800,000 is income-producing and the full interest is deductible—provided both properties remain available for rent. Your records should show the settlement statements and how the funds were disbursed. One Loan, Investment + Private Suppose you borrow $700,000 and allocate $600,000 to a rental property and $100,000 to renovate your main residence. Only (600,000 ÷ 700,000) of the interest is deductible. As you repay the loan, the ATO presumes repayments reduce both purposes pro rata unless you can prove otherwise. Advanced structuring—such as asking the lender to split the facility into two accounts at the outset—avoids this headache. Refinancing to "Cash Out" When you refinance an investment property to access equity, deductibility depends on what you do with the extra funds. Refinancing from $400,000 to $600,000 and using the $200,000 to buy another rental keeps the full balance deductible. Using that $200,000 for a boat means only $400,000 of the principal supports an income-producing purpose, and interest must be apportioned accordingly. --- Construction Loans and Capitalised Interest Interest incurred during construction of a rental property is deductible under section 25-25 of the ITAA 1997, even though the property is not yet producing income. The rule applies from the day construction begins until it is complete and available for rent. Key points: Capitalised interest (added to the loan rather than paid in cash) is still deductible in the year it is incurred, subject to the non‑commercial loss rules if you have other income sources. Once construction finishes, you must offer the property for rent. If you leave it vacant for personal reasons—waiting for a family member to move in, for instance—further interest becomes non-deductible. Land-holding costs (interest on the land component before construction starts) are also deductible under section 25-25, provided you intend to build a rental property. If you cannot substantiate that a loan or a portion of it was used to earn assessable income, the deduction will be denied. Essential records: 1. Loan contract and facility limit changes – shows the initial advance and any subsequent increases or redraws. 2. Bank statements – trace the funds from drawdown to their end use (settlement agent, vendor, offset top-up, personal account transfer). 3. Settlement statements – for property purchases, showing the deposit, balance due and how the loan funded the shortfall. 4. Redraw logs – if you've redrawn, keep a spreadsheet showing date, amount and purpose, with receipts for non-deductible expenses. 5. Apportionment workings – a running calculation each financial year showing the income-producing and private portions of the outstanding balance. The ATO's data-matching picks up loan interest claimed on your return and compares it to bank reporting. A $35,000 interest deduction on a $500,000 loan at 5.5 % looks reasonable; the same $35,000 on a $400,000 loan after a $100,000 redraw will trigger questions unless you can show the redraw bought another rental property. Interest is deductible inside the fund to the extent it is incurred in gaining assessable income—rent, in most cases. However: The sole-purpose test (section 62 SIS Act ) requires that the asset is maintained to provide retirement benefits. Using a redraw for trustee personal expenses breaches super law and may trigger penalties far worse than losing a tax deduction. Any interest apportionment must be tracked at the fund level; the SMSF's auditor will scrutinise loan statements every year. Family Trusts and Companies A discretionary trust or company that borrows to buy rental property claims interest deductions in its own return. The same purpose-test rules apply, but the entity structure adds a wrinkle: Unpaid present entitlements (UPEs): If the trust distributes income to beneficiaries but doesn't pay cash, it may lend the UPE back to beneficiaries. If those beneficiaries spend the funds privately, the trust's original loan remains deductible (because the trust's purpose was to buy the rental), but the UPE loan in the hands of the beneficiary is private and non-deductible. Director loans and Division 7A: A company that lends to a shareholder may trigger a deemed dividend under Division 7A unless a complying loan agreement is in place. The company's original investment-loan interest is still deductible; the shareholder's use of a Division 7A loan is typically private. If your trust has made interest-free UPE loans to beneficiaries who then spent the money on holidays, your actual position will differ. --- What This Means for Your Portfolio Rental property loan interest is deductible when—and only when—the borrowed funds produce assessable income.
