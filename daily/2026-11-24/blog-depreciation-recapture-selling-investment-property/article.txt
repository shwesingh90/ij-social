Depreciation Recapture Selling Investment Property | InvestorJoint + Tax · 22 Nov 2026 · 8 min read Depreciation Recapture When You Sell: What Australian Investors Owe If you've claimed depreciation on plant & equipment, a balancing adjustment under Division 40 can add thousands to your tax bill at settlement. Here's how it works and what it means for your sale proceeds. When you sell an investment property in Australia, the ATO doesn't simply let you keep every dollar of depreciation deductions you've claimed over the years. Instead, Division 40 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (ITAA 1997) triggers what's known as a balancing adjustment on plant and equipment—an offset that can add $5,000 to $20,000 or more to your taxable income in the year of sale. The rule is straightforward: if you've written down an asset's value and then dispose of it for more than its written-down value, you include the difference as assessable income. Here's how depreciation recapture works in practice, the most common misconceptions, and what you need to calculate before you sign a contract of sale. What depreciation recapture actually means in Australia Australia doesn't use the American term "depreciation recapture." Instead, the ATO calls it a balancing adjustment . Under Division 40 of the ITAA 1997, when you dispose of a depreciating asset—such as the dishwasher, air conditioner, or carpet in your rental property—the tax office compares two figures: 1. Termination value : typically the market value attributed to that asset at sale, or a reasonable portion of the total sale price. 2. Adjustable value (also called written-down value or tax book value): the asset's original cost minus all depreciation deductions you've claimed. If the termination value exceeds the adjustable value, the excess is treated as assessable income in the year of disposal. If the adjustable value exceeds the termination value, you may be able to claim a balancing deduction—though for property investors, termination values on well-maintained plant and equipment often exceed the written-down figure, triggering income inclusion rather than a further deduction. This adjustment sits alongside your capital gains tax (CGT) calculation; it doesn't replace it. You still calculate a capital gain (or loss) on the property as a whole, but the balancing adjustment for each individual depreciating asset is carved out and taxed as ordinary income, not as a capital gain. That means no 50 per cent CGT discount applies to balancing adjustments if you're an individual or trust. Division 40 balancing adjustments: the mechanics Plant and equipment—Division 40 assets—include anything that isn't permanently fixed to the structure: ovens, blinds, carpets, hot-water systems, split-system air conditioners, ceiling fans, and so on. When you commissioned a quantity surveyor's depreciation schedule, these items were listed in the "low-value pool" or "general pool" section with diminishing-value or prime-cost write-off rates. Here's a worked example. Suppose you bought an investment unit in Sydney in July 2018 for $650,000. Your quantity surveyor identified $40,000 in plant and equipment. Over six years to June 2024, you claimed $22,000 in total depreciation deductions on those assets, leaving an adjustable value of $18,000 ($40,000 − $22,000). You sell the unit in September 2024 for $780,000. Your solicitor's contract apportions $25,000 of the sale price to the chattels and removable fixtures—the termination value . The balancing adjustment is: $25,000 (termination) − $18,000 (adjustable) = $7,000 assessable income That $7,000 is added to your taxable income for FY 2024–25 and taxed at your marginal rate. If you're in the 37 per cent bracket (plus 2 per cent Medicare levy), the adjustment costs roughly $2,730 in extra tax in the year of sale. Meanwhile, the building structure—capital works under Division 43—continues to form part of your CGT cost base and is not subject to a balancing adjustment. Common misconceptions about clawback Myth 1: "All depreciation is clawed back when I sell." Only plant and equipment (Division 40) triggers a balancing adjustment, and only to the extent termination value exceeds adjustable value. The 2.5 per cent annual capital works deduction you claimed on the building shell (Division 43) is never clawed back; instead, it reduces your CGT cost base, which increases your capital gain. The economic effect is different: a reduced cost base means more capital gain (eligible for the 50 per cent discount for individuals), whereas a balancing adjustment is ordinary income taxed in full. Myth 2: "I can avoid the adjustment by not declaring the asset values in the contract." If the contract is silent, the ATO will apportion the sale price according to market value under section 40-300. In practice, the Commissioner expects a reasonable estimate, and your solicitor or conveyancer will often include a chattels schedule. Failing to declare values doesn't eliminate the obligation—it just shifts the burden of proof onto you if the ATO issues an amendment. Myth 3: "Investors who bought after 9 May 2017 don't have balancing adjustments." The 9 May 2017 budget measure stopped new owners from claiming depreciation on second-hand plant and equipment, but it didn't remove the balancing-adjustment rules. If you purchased before that date—or installed brand-new assets after settlement—you still trigger a balancing adjustment on disposal. If you bought after 9 May 2017 and never claimed depreciation on the previous owner's chattels, your adjustable value for those items is effectively zero, so the entire apportioned termination value may be assessable income (a harsher outcome in many cases). Myth 4: "The adjustment only applies if I made a profit overall." Balancing adjustments are calculated asset by asset, independent of whether the property as a whole gained or lost value. Even if you sell at a capital loss, you can still have assessable income from balancing adjustments on individual items that have appreciated relative to their written-down value. How to calculate your exposure before settlement The simplest approach: 1. Retrieve your original depreciation schedule (the report from your quantity surveyor, often 40–60 pages). 2. Roll forward the adjustable value for each pooled or individually tracked asset to the expected date of settlement. Your accountant can do this, or InvestorJoint's depreciation calculator will project future deductions and track written-down values across financial years. 3. Obtain a termination-value apportionment . Some solicitors or conveyancers will prepare a chattels schedule; others leave it to the buyer and seller to agree. A conservative rule of thumb is 3–8 per cent of the sale price for a residential unit or house, depending on fit-out quality and age. 4. Subtract adjustable value from termination value for each asset (or pool). Sum any positive differences to estimate total assessable income. 5. Multiply by your marginal tax rate (including Medicare levy and any state-based surcharges) to forecast the cash impact. Run this calculation before you accept an offer. If the balancing adjustment is material—say, $15,000 assessable income adding $6,000 to your tax bill—you'll want that cash on hand in the financial year of settlement, rather than scrambling when your accountant lodges your return eight months later. Interaction with CGT and the 50 per cent discount Balancing adjustments and capital gains are computed separately, but both affect your total tax in the year of sale. Step 1: Calculate the CGT event Your capital proceeds are the sale price (less any amount attributed to trading stock or revenue assets). Your cost base includes the original purchase price, stamp duty, legal fees, and capital improvements—but is reduced by any Division 43 capital works deductions you've claimed. Apply the 50 per cent discount if you're an individual or trust and you've held the property for more than twelve months. Trusts must apply the discount before distributing, and beneficiaries receive a "discounted" amount. Step 2: Add the Division 40 balancing adjustment This sits outside the CGT regime. It is ordinary income, included in your assessable income at step 1 of the tax calculation. No 50 per cent discount applies. If you're a company or SMSF in pension phase, the lack of discount may matter less (companies don't get the CGT discount anyway; SMSFs in pension phase pay zero tax on both). Step 3: Combine and gross-up for tax Your total taxable income for the year includes salary, rent, net capital gain (post-discount), and the full balancing adjustment. Marginal rates then apply. For a Sydney professional in the 45 per cent bracket selling a property with a $120,000 net capital gain (before discount) and a $10,000 balancing adjustment, the incremental tax might be: Net capital gain: $120,000 × 50% = $60,000 × 47% (incl. Medicare + Budget Repair Levy if applicable in earlier years, but let's use current 45% + 2%) = $28,200 Balancing adjustment: $10,000 × 47% = $4,700 Total incremental tax: $32,900 The balancing adjustment alone accounts for 14 per cent of the total CGT-year tax slug in this example. Strategies to minimise balancing adjustments Australian tax law offers limited scope to avoid balancing adjustments, but a few tactics are worth noting: 1. Delay the sale into the next financial year if you expect lower income (e.g., retirement, parental leave, or a gap year). The balancing adjustment and capital gain will be taxed at a lower marginal rate. 2. Negotiate a lower termination value in the contract of sale. If the buyer agrees to apportion less value to chattels (and more to land and structure), your balancing adjustment shrinks. This is a commercial negotiation—buyers may resist if they want to claim higher depreciation going forward (though post-9 May 2017 buyers get no deduction on second-hand plant anyway). 3. Replace high-value items before sale . If the oven or hot-water system is near end-of-life, replacing it shortly before listing gives you a final-year deduction at cost, and the brand-new item will have a termination value close to its adjustable value, minimising the gap. This only makes sense if you'd replace it anyway; spending $2,000 to save $600 in balancing-adjustment tax is poor economics. 4. Consider selling the property inside an SMSF in pension phase . Both the capital gain and the balancing adjustment are tax-free in that environment. However, restructuring into an SMSF solely to sell is expensive (stamp duty on transfer in, borrowing restrictions, compliance costs) and rarely worth it unless you were planning the SMSF strategy years in advance. 5. Document partial disposals and scrap . If you removed and discarded assets (e.g., ripped out old carpet and sent it to landfill), you triggered a balancing event at that time. Provided you kept records and lodged the adjustment in the year of disposal, those items won't create a second adjustment at property sale. Few investors bother with this for low-value items, but it's technically correct. For multi-property portfolios, timing the sale of a loss-making property in the same financial year as a profitable one can offset the net capital gain, though balancing adjustments (being ordinary income) won't be offset by a capital loss. Record-keeping and ATO scrutiny The ATO's data-matching program links property settlements (reported by your solicitor or conveyancer under the foreign-resident CGT withholding regime and other reporting) with your tax return. If you lodge a return showing a capital gain but no balancing adjustment—and the ATO's records suggest significant plant and equipment was present—expect a please-explain letter or an amendment. Keep these documents for at least five years after the sale: Original quantity surveyor's depreciation schedule Annual reconciliation of adjustable values (your accountant should maintain this, or pull the data straight from your InvestorJoint ledger) Contract of sale, including any chattels schedule or annexure listing removable fixtures Receipts for any capital improvements or replacements (these create new depreciating assets with their own adjustable values) Evidence of disposals before settlement (e.g., photos, tradie invoices for removal) If you're part of a trust or company structure, the same rules apply—the entity that claimed the depreciation must include the balancing adjustment in its assessable income. For discretionary trusts, this will flow through to beneficiaries as ordinary trust income in their individual returns. For companies, it increases corporate taxable income at the flat 25 per cent or 30 per cent rate. SMSFs report both the capital gain and balancing adjustment in their annual return; the trustee's tax rate (0 per cent in pension phase, 15 per cent in accumulation) then applies. What this means for your portfolio Depreciation is one of the most powerful levers in negative gearing , turning paper write-downs into real cash-flow relief every year. But the Australian tax system ensures you don't double-dip: deductions claimed along the way are reconciled on disposal, and any "excess" depreciation—where the termination value proves you wrote the asset down too far—comes back as assessable income.
