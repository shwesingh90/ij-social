Here's what changes when you choose tenants in common over joint tenancy. The Bottom Line: How Ownership Structure Shapes Tax and Inheritance When you buy investment property with another person—a spouse, business partner, or family member—the distinction between tenants in common and joint tenants determines three critical outcomes: who inherits your share when you die, whether you can hold unequal ownership percentages, and how the ATO treats rental income and capital gains for tax. Joint tenants carry automatic survivorship rights (your share passes to the co-owner, bypassing your will), while tenants in common lets each party bequeath their percentage independently and hold unequal stakes—80/20, 60/40, or any split that suits your circumstances. Most investors choose tenants in common because it aligns income distribution with the lower-earning partner's marginal tax rate, preserves estate planning flexibility, and avoids the risk of unintended inheritance if a relationship breaks down. This post unpacks the legal mechanics, tax treatment under Division 6 and s.86 ITAA 1997, and practical scenarios where each structure makes sense for FY2024–25 and beyond. Joint Tenancy: Survivorship and Equal Ownership Only Joint tenancy (sometimes called "joint proprietorship" on older titles) is a form of co-ownership where all parties hold an undivided, equal interest in the entire property. The hallmark feature is the right of survivorship : when one joint tenant dies, their interest automatically vests in the surviving joint tenant(s), regardless of what the deceased's will says. Key characteristics: Equal shares mandated. You cannot be joint tenants with a 70/30 split; each party must own an identical percentage. For two owners, that's 50/50; for three, it's one-third each. Four unities. At common law, joint tenancy requires unity of possession (equal right to occupy), unity of interest (same type and duration of interest), unity of title (acquired under the same instrument), and unity of time (interests vest simultaneously). Survivorship overrides wills. On death, the deceased's share does not form part of their estate. It passes by operation of law to the surviving joint tenant(s), so any bequest in a will directing that share elsewhere is ineffective. Severance converts to tenancy in common. A joint tenant can unilaterally sever the joint tenancy by notice, bankruptcy, or disposing of their interest, transforming the arrangement into tenants in common. Joint tenancy is common for principal residences held by married couples who want simplicity and certainty that the survivor retains the home without probate delays. For investment property, however, the inflexibility around income splitting and estate planning often outweighs the administrative convenience. Tenants in Common: Unequal Shares and Independent Inheritance Tenants in common is co-ownership where each party holds a distinct, divisible share—expressed as a percentage or fraction on the title—that can be bequeathed, sold, or mortgaged independently. Key characteristics: Unequal ownership permitted. You can register any split: 99/1, 80/20, 60/40. The only requirement is that percentages total 100%. No right of survivorship. Each owner's share passes according to their will (or intestacy rules if they die without one). This means you control who inherits your interest—children, a discretionary trust, or a nominated beneficiary. Income and expenses follow legal title. The ATO taxes rental income and allows deductions in proportion to each co-owner's registered percentage, provided that split reflects economic reality. If one party contributes more capital or services, the arrangement must be documented to withstand ATO scrutiny under TR 93/32 and TR 2004/D25. Partition or sale by court order. If co-owners disagree, any tenant in common can apply under the Property Law Act (varies by state) to force a sale or physical partition, subject to equitable considerations. Tenants in common is the default choice for investors seeking tax efficiency, estate certainty, or joint ventures with non-spouse partners where equal ownership doesn't reflect economic contributions. How Rental Income and Deductions Are Split for Tax The ATO's position is straightforward: rental income and expenses are attributed according to legal ownership on the title , not according to who paid the deposit, services the loan, or manages the property day-to-day. Example: Mia and Tom buy a $750,000 investment unit in Brisbane as 70/30 tenants in common. The property generates $32,000 annual rent and incurs $18,000 in interest, $3,200 in rates and strata, and $2,800 in depreciation. Mia (70% owner) reports $22,400 gross rental income and claims $16,800 in deductions (70% × $24,000). Tom (30% owner) reports $9,600 gross income and claims $7,200 in deductions. If Mia is on the 45% marginal rate and Tom is on 32.5%, this split delivers a combined after-tax saving compared to 50/50 joint tenancy. Use the negative gearing calculator to model different ownership ratios against your marginal rates. What if we contributed unequally to the deposit? Legal title governs tax allocation. If Tom paid 50% of the deposit but holds only 30% title, he cannot claim 50% of deductions unless the ownership percentage is updated via a formal transfer (which triggers stamp duty in most states). Informal agreements or side letters do not change the ATO's view. See TR 93/32 for the distinction between "ordinary income from property" and partnerships. Joint tenancy default: always 50/50. If you hold as joint tenants, the ATO treats each party as a 50% owner for all income and deduction purposes, regardless of who services the loan or pays body corporate fees. You cannot direct a larger share of rental income to the lower-earning spouse without severing the joint tenancy and converting to tenants in common. Capital Gains Tax: Cost Base and Disposal by Share When you sell (or are deemed to sell under a CGT event), each co-owner calculates their own cost base and capital gain based on their ownership percentage and the timing of acquisition. Joint tenants: Each owner's cost base includes 50% of the original purchase price, 50% of acquisition costs (stamp duty, legals), and 50% of capital improvements. On sale, each reports 50% of the net capital gain. The 50% CGT discount (for assets held >12 months) applies individually if the owner is an Australian resident individual or trust. Tenants in common: Cost base and capital gain are calculated per registered share. If Sarah owns 80% and her brother owns 20% of a Sydney investment flat acquired in September 2018 for $820,000 (plus $33,000 stamp duty), Sarah's cost base is 80% × $853,000 = $682,400. On a June 2025 sale for $1.05 million (net proceeds $1,030,000 after agent fees), Sarah's gross gain is 80% × ($1,030,000 − $853,000) = $141,600, eligible for the 50% discount if she's held >12 months as an individual. Change of ownership mid-holding period: If joint tenants sever and convert to tenants in common with a different split, no CGT event occurs provided there is no change in beneficial ownership—that is, you're simply documenting an existing arrangement. However, if one party acquires an additional share from the other (e.g., moving from 50/50 to 70/30), the transferring party triggers CGT event A1 on the percentage sold, and the acquiring party establishes a new cost base for the additional slice at market value on the transfer date. Stamp duty also applies on the consideration for the transferred share in NSW, Victoria, Queensland, and other states—check the relevant stamp duty calculator for your jurisdiction. Estate Planning: Wills, Probate, and Blended Families Joint tenancy and survivorship risk: Automatic survivorship means the surviving joint tenant receives the deceased's share immediately, outside the estate. This can be advantageous for speed—no grant of probate is needed to update the title—but it can also defeat your intentions: Blended families: If you have children from a previous marriage and hold property as joint tenants with a new spouse, your share will pass to the spouse on your death, potentially excluding your children entirely if the spouse does not leave the property to them in turn. Second marriages: A surviving spouse may remarry and re-title the property as joint tenants with the new partner, further diluting your children's inheritance. Creditor claims: Because the share passes outside probate, it may be easier for creditors of the deceased to pursue the survivor's newly augmented interest if debts remain unsatisfied. Tenants in common and testamentary control: Each owner's share forms part of their estate and passes according to their will. This allows you to: Bequeath your percentage to children, a testamentary trust, or other beneficiaries. Quarantine your share from a surviving co-owner's future relationships. Nominate a life interest (e.g., your spouse can live in the property for life, then it passes to your children on their death). Equalise inheritance across multiple children by leaving unequal property shares or offsetting with other assets. Practical scenario: Alex and Jordan (de facto couple) buy a $950,000 investment property in Perth as 60/40 tenants in common. Alex contributes 60% of the deposit and wants to leave her share to her two adult children. Jordan wants his 40% to pass to his sister. Both draft wills specifying these bequests. If they had held as joint tenants, Alex's death would transfer her 60% to Jordan automatically, disinheriting her children unless Jordan's will then directed a portion back—a fragile and uncertain chain. For SMSF trustees considering property inside super, note that the fund's trust deed and binding death benefit nomination govern distribution, not the land title ownership structure. See the SMSF guide for details. When to Choose Joint Tenancy vs Tenants in Common Choose joint tenancy if: You want automatic survivorship and trust the co-owner to manage the asset after your death. You are married/de facto with no children from prior relationships and wish to simplify probate. Equal ownership (50/50) aligns with your contributions and tax planning goals. You value administrative simplicity and do not foresee needing to adjust ownership percentages. Choose tenants in common if: You want to split income to the lower-earning partner's tax rate (e.g., 70/30 instead of 50/50). You have children from a previous relationship or blended-family considerations. You are buying with a business partner, sibling, or friend and contributions or risk tolerance differ. You want testamentary control over who inherits your share. One party's marginal tax rate is significantly higher, and unequal ownership would deliver material savings. Run scenarios through the trust vs personal calculator to compare effective rates across structures. You may need to adjust ownership percentages in future (noting stamp duty and CGT implications). Can you switch from one to the other? Yes. Joint tenants can sever unilaterally by written notice (lodged with the land titles office) or by mutual agreement, converting to tenants in common. The reverse—consolidating tenants in common into joint tenancy—requires all parties to execute a new transfer and satisfy the four unities. Both directions can attract stamp duty if consideration changes hands; seek legal advice before proceeding. Multi-Entity Portfolios: Trusts, Companies, and Mixed Structures Many investors hold property across a mix of entities—some in personal names (as tenants in common), others in a family trust, an SMSF, or a company. The choice of co-ownership structure interacts with these entities: Personal + trust co-ownership: You (individual) and your discretionary trust can hold a property as tenants in common, say 50/50. Your 50% flows to your personal tax return at your marginal rate; the trust's 50% is distributed to beneficiaries according to the trustee's resolution each year. This blends flexibility (trust distributions) with the CGT main residence exemption or 50% discount (if you later move in). SMSF + personal: Generally not permitted. Under s.66 SIS Act and SMSF rules, a fund cannot acquire an asset from a related party except in narrow circumstances (business real property, listed securities, in-specie transfers under specific conditions). Co-ownership with a member personally would breach the sole-purpose test. The property must be 100% owned by the SMSF trustees as trustees for the fund. Company co-ownership: Companies can hold as tenants in common with individuals or other entities. The company's share of rental income is taxed at the 25% (base rate entity) or 30% (other) rate, with no 50% CGT discount. This is less tax-efficient for capital growth but may suit trading or development strategies. See the company purchase guide for when corporate ownership makes sense. Deceased estate as tenant in common: If a tenant in common dies, their executor becomes the legal owner of that share pending distribution under the will. The title will show "The executor of the estate of [Name]" as tenant in common with the surviving co-owner(s). Until the executor transfers or sells, rental income from the deceased's share is estate income, taxed under Division 6 of the ITAA 1936.
