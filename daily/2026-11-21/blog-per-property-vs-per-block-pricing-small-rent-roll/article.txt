Per-Property vs Per-Block Pricing for a Small Rent Roll + Pm · 18 Nov 2026 · 6 min read Per-Property vs Per-Block Pricing for a Small Rent Roll Why block pricing is awkward just above a block multiple and for tiny portfolios, with worked examples and how to model cost as your rent roll grows. Block pricing charges you for a bundle of properties at a time, and it behaves awkwardly in two places: just above a multiple of the block size, and when your portfolio is smaller than one block. Per-property pricing charges for each property you manage, so the cost follows your rent roll more closely. Neither structure is better in the abstract; the right question is which one fits the number of properties you have now and the number you expect to have in a year. Two pricing structures, plainly Per-property (or per-door) pricing. You pay a fixed amount for each property you manage. Add one property, and the bill goes up by one property's price. Block pricing. You pay for tiers or bundles, for example "up to 50 properties", "up to 100 properties". The price steps up when you cross a tier boundary. Many vendors blend the two, with a base fee plus a per-property amount, or tiers with minimums. The point of this post is the arithmetic of the pure forms, so you can spot how a real price list behaves. Where block pricing gets awkward Just above a multiple of the block size The illustration below uses round numbers chosen for clarity. They are an example only and are not any vendor's price or block size. Suppose a vendor sells blocks of 50 properties, and you pay for whole blocks. | Properties managed | Blocks you pay for | Properties you are paying for but not using | |---|---|---| | 50 | 1 | 0 | | 51 | 2 | 49 | | 75 | 2 | 25 | | 100 | 2 | 0 | | 101 | 3 | 49 | At 50 properties you use the block fully. Add one more and you pay for a second block, 49 of whose places are empty. Your cost for that one extra property is the price of a whole block. The effective cost per property is lowest at exactly a multiple of the block size and spikes at the next property. This matters most in the moment a small agency is growing, because winning one more management can add a step in cost rather than a slope. It also encourages odd behaviour, such as hesitating to take a 51st management or hunting for a way to stay under a threshold. For very small portfolios The same logic bites hardest below the first block. Suppose the smallest tier covers 50 properties and you manage 12. You pay for a tier sized for 50, so you pay for 38 properties you do not have. Your cost per property is the block price divided by 12, which is far higher than it would be at 50. For a new agency, or a landlord-turned-manager with a handful of properties, a minimum tier can be a large share of revenue. If the agency is growing, the tier might be right in six months and wrong now. If it is not growing, you are paying a permanent premium. Where block pricing is the better fit To be fair to blocks: if your portfolio sits comfortably in the middle of a tier and is stable, block pricing gives you a predictable bill with no per-property surprises. It can also work out cheaper per property than a per-property rate once you are near the top of a block. The trouble is only at the edges, which is exactly where a small, growing agency lives. How a small agency should model cost growth Do not compare price lists at your current size alone. Model the cost at the sizes you expect to be. 1. Pick three portfolio sizes. Your current number, your likely number in 12 months, and an optimistic number. For example, 12, 25 and 40 properties. 2. Work out the total cost at each size under each structure. Use total monthly cost, not the headline per-property figure. 3. Find the steps. For block pricing, list every size at which the cost jumps, and note how many properties above a boundary you would be at your likely growth path. 4. Calculate the cost of the next property. The marginal cost of the 13th, 26th or 41st property is what you actually face when you take on a new management. 5. Check what else is priced separately. Setup, onboarding, trust accounting modules, user seats and payment fees can all sit outside the headline price. Ask what the all-in monthly cost is. 6. Include the cost of change. If you outgrow a tier and have to move, what does switching cost in time and data? Our switching cost calculator helps you estimate it. Whichever way you model it, check each vendor's current published pricing yourself, and confirm with your accountant how GST applies to your software costs.
