Rent Arrears Process in Australia: The Escalation Ladder by State + Leasing · 18 Nov 2026 · 11 min read Rent Arrears Process in Australia: The Escalation Ladder by State The general rent arrears escalation ladder in Australia, from early contact to a tribunal application, with the forum, notice trigger and enforcement rules for each state. The rent arrears process in Australia follows the same general ladder in every state: contact the tenant early, agree a payment plan if one is realistic, issue a formal notice if the arrears continue, and apply to the tribunal or court if the notice is not complied with. The forum, the forms and the day counts differ by state, so confirm each step with your state's tenancy authority before acting. A manager cannot end a tenancy for arrears without following that state's process. This is general information, not legal advice. Rules and forms change. The figures below were checked against the official state and territory pages in November 2026, and you should check the current page for your state before you rely on any number, because a wrong form or miscounted day can invalidate a notice. The general escalation ladder Step 1: Early contact The first step is the same everywhere and costs nothing: contact the tenant as soon as a payment is missed. A call, text or email asking what happened resolves many cases. Keep a dated record of every attempt. Early contact also gives you information about whether this is a one-off, a cash flow issue or something more serious. For day-to-day habits, see rent arrears management best practices . Step 2: Reminder and written notice of arrears If the first contact does not work, send a written reminder that states the amount owing and the dates it covers. This is usually not a formal legal notice, but it creates a clear record. The formal notice stage that follows has its own forms and periods in every state, set out in the table below. Step 3: Payment plan If the tenant has a genuine reason and wants to catch up, a payment plan can be better for everyone than a tribunal application. Put it in writing, with amounts and dates, and get the owner's agreement first, because the owner is the party who bears the loss. In several states a tenant who pays what is owing, or keeps to a repayment plan agreed with the landlord, can avoid a termination order (NSW, Victoria and WA say so on their official pages), and the ACT tribunal can make a payment order instead of terminating. How a plan interacts with a notice you have already served differs by state, so check before you rely on one. Step 4: Formal notice If arrears continue, the next step is a formal notice that tells the tenant to pay within a set time or face a termination application. The trigger point, the form, the notice period and how it must be served are all set by state law, and they are summarised in the table below. Using the wrong form, miscounting the days or serving it incorrectly are the commonest reasons a later application fails. Step 5: Tribunal or court application If the tenant does not pay or reach an agreement after the notice, the landlord or agent can apply to the relevant tribunal or court for a termination or possession order. Only that order, and the enforcement that follows it, ends the tenancy. A manager must not change locks, remove belongings, cut utilities or harass the tenant. Victoria says in terms that rental providers cannot lock out renters or personally carry out an eviction, and WA says a landlord must never force a tenant out or change the locks without a court order. Who carries out enforcement of an order is covered in the table below. Step 6: Recovery Arrears owing after the tenancy ends, and costs beyond the bond, are a separate recovery question, and the bond claim process differs by state, so check your state's bond authority. See bond claims: wear and tear versus damage and bond lodgement requirements . Tenancy databases and hardship Listing a tenant on a tenancy database is tightly controlled and is not a step in the arrears ladder. In NSW, listing is only allowed once the tenancy has ended, and rent arrears alone or a termination notice are not grounds for it. In several states a tenant must be told and given at least 14 days to respond or object before a listing (NSW, Victoria, WA, SA and Tasmania), and in the Northern Territory the period is 28 days. Check the rules in your state before you list anyone. Hardship and family violence protections also exist and can affect arrears action. For example, VCAT can postpone an eviction for up to 30 days in cases of serious hardship, and the NT Tribunal can suspend a possession order for up to 90 days for severe hardship. Your state's tenancy authority explains the current protections. Mistakes that derail an arrears case Waiting too long before the first contact. Taking part payments without recording what they are for, which can muddy the arrears figure. Issuing a notice using the wrong form or counting days incorrectly. Verbal promises that are not recorded. Ignoring hardship or family violence circumstances that the state rules treat specially. Treating the tribunal as the first step instead of the last. Frequently asked questions How many days behind before I can issue a notice? It depends on the state. As checked against the official pages in November 2026: NSW more than 14 days behind, Victoria more than 14 days late (7 for a rooming house or caravan park), Queensland more than 7 days behind (Form 11), South Australia at least 14 days unpaid, the ACT 1 week unpaid, and the Northern Territory not less than 14 days. In WA you can give a breach notice or a termination notice without one, depending on the form. Tasmania uses a notice to vacate and we could not confirm an arrears-days threshold from an official page. Always check the current page for your state, because these figures can change. Can I evict a tenant for arrears without a tribunal order? No. In general, a tenancy ends through the state's formal process and an order, not through the manager's own action. Victoria and WA say plainly that a landlord cannot lock a tenant out or carry out an eviction, and enforcement of a possession order is carried out by police in Victoria and Queensland, a Sheriff's Officer in NSW and a bailiff in WA and SA. Check how it works in your state. Should I offer a payment plan? Often it is worth trying, with the owner's agreement and in writing. NSW, Victoria and WA say on their official pages that a tenant who pays what is owing, or keeps to an agreed repayment plan, may avoid a termination order. Whether a plan affects a notice already issued varies by state, so check before you rely on one. Does InvestorJoint issue arrears notices?
