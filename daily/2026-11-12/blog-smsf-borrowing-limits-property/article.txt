SMSF Borrowing Limits Property: LRBA Rules & Caps | InvestorJoint + Structure · 11 Nov 2026 · 8 min read SMSF Property Loans: Limited Recourse Borrowing Rules and Limits SMSFs can borrow to buy property under strict limited recourse rules. Here's how LRBAs work, what lenders will actually approve, and the structural traps most trustees miss. Self-managed super funds have been able to borrow to acquire property since 2007, but the mechanics are nothing like a standard home loan. An SMSF can only borrow under a limited recourse borrowing arrangement (LRBA), which walls off the asset in a bare trust, caps the lender's recourse to that single property, and imposes strict rules on improvements, replacements and refinancing. Most major banks will lend up to 80 % LVR for residential property held in an SMSF—though in practice many stop at 70 %—and commercial property loans typically max out at 70 % LVR . You cannot cross-collateralise with personal assets, you cannot use offset accounts linked to the LRBA, and the loan must satisfy the sole-purpose test at every stage. Miss any of these details and the ATO can treat the arrangement as non-complying, triggering a 45 % tax rate on the fund's entire income and assets. What is a limited recourse borrowing arrangement? A limited recourse borrowing arrangement is the only lawful way for an SMSF to borrow money to acquire an asset under Section 67A of the Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993 . The legislation allows a complying fund to borrow provided: 1. The borrowed funds are used to acquire a single acquirable asset (or a collection of identical assets with the same market value that would ordinarily be acquired together, such as a parcel of shares). 2. The asset is held on trust—typically a bare trust—for the SMSF until the loan is fully repaid. 3. The lender's rights are limited to the asset itself in the event of default; the lender has no recourse to the other assets of the SMSF. 4. The SMSF has a beneficial interest in the asset and the right to acquire legal title once the debt is discharged. The bare trust is not a discretionary or unit trust—it exists purely to hold legal title on behalf of the SMSF trustee. The SMSF trustee is usually also the appointor of the bare trust, ensuring control. The lender takes security over both the asset held in the bare trust and an assignment of the SMSF's beneficial interest. Because recourse is limited, most lenders price SMSF loans 50–100 basis points higher than equivalent owner-occupier or standard investment loans, and they scrutinise the fund's cash flow, contribution history, and member age profiles more closely than a personal application. Hard and soft limits: what lenders will actually approve There is no legislated loan-to-value-ratio cap in the SIS Act. The 80 % LVR ceiling for residential property and 70 % for commercial property are lender credit policies, not ATO rules. In practice: Residential property : Major lenders (CBA, Westpac, NAB, ANZ via intermediary channels) typically offer up to 80 % LVR, though many will only go to 70 % for SMSF borrowers without lenders mortgage insurance—which is rarely available for super funds. Commercial property : Most specialists cap at 70 % LVR. The property must be investment-grade (no special-purpose buildings) and leased or have strong lease-up prospects. Vacant land : Almost impossible to finance under an LRBA. Construction loans are also rare because improvements to the asset can breach the "single acquirable asset" rule if not carefully documented. Serviceability is assessed on the fund's existing balance, projected contributions (capped at the concessional and non-concessional limits for members), rental income, and any pension drawdowns. Lenders do not consider personal income of members; the SMSF must service the loan from its own cash flows. Funds with members approaching pension phase or already drawing account-based pensions face tighter serviceability tests because projected outflows rise. A rule of thumb from specialist SMSF lenders: the fund should have at least $200,000 in liquid assets after the deposit and costs, and total super balances (across all members) exceeding $500,000 before contemplating a property LRBA. Smaller funds can qualify, but margins for error shrink. The bare trust structure and what happens at settlement On settlement day, the SMSF does not take immediate legal title. Instead: 1. The SMSF trustee (or corporate trustee) establishes a bare trust with a separate trust deed, naming itself or a related corporate entity as trustee of the bare trust. 2. The lender advances funds to the bare trust trustee, which uses those funds—together with cash contributed by the SMSF—to purchase the property. 3. Legal title is registered in the name of the bare trust trustee (often recorded as "XYZ Pty Ltd as trustee for the ABC SMSF Bare Trust" ). 4. The SMSF holds the beneficial interest and makes loan repayments from fund cash flow. 5. Once the loan is repaid, title is transferred from the bare trust to the SMSF trustee at nil or nominal stamp duty (most states exempt or heavily discount in-specie transfers within super). All holding costs—council rates, insurance, repairs, strata fees—are paid by the SMSF and recorded in the fund's accounts. The bare trust itself does not lodge a tax return; it is transparent. Rental income and deductions flow through to the SMSF's annual return. The security package usually includes: First-registered mortgage over the property held by the bare trust. Charge or assignment over the SMSF's beneficial interest. Sometimes a guarantee limited to the value of the property (to satisfy limited recourse requirements). If the SMSF defaults, the lender can only seize and sell the property in the bare trust. Other fund assets—shares, cash, other properties owned outright—are quarantined. This quarantine is why lenders charge a premium and why cross-collateralisation with personal property or other SMSF assets is prohibited under Section 67A. Improvements, replacements and refinancing traps A mortgage is not set-and-forget. Three common changes can accidentally breach the LRBA rules: Improvements and repairs Repairs and maintenance funded from the SMSF's own cash are fine. Replacing a hot-water system, repainting, or fixing a roof are allowable because they maintain the asset's character. Capital improvements —adding a second storey, subdividing the block, building a granny flat— cannot be funded by increasing the LRBA loan . Section 67A(1)(c) requires the borrowed money to acquire a single acquirable asset. ATO guidance (including Law Companion Ruling LCR 2010/1 ) states that improvements change the character of the original asset, meaning you are no longer holding the same "single acquirable asset." If you want to renovate, the SMSF must fund the work from existing cash reserves or new contributions. Some lenders will allow a separate, non-LRBA loan for improvements, but this requires careful structuring and most SMSF auditors will insist on written ATO private rulings if the arrangement is novel. Replacement assets You cannot swap the property in the bare trust for a different property while the LRBA is outstanding. Selling the original asset and using the same loan to buy another is a breach. If you want to sell and buy elsewhere, you must: 1. Repay the existing LRBA in full (from sale proceeds or fund cash). 2. Establish a new LRBA and new bare trust for the replacement property. This can trigger capital gains tax (though taxed at 10 % if the asset was held >12 months and the fund is in accumulation phase, or 0 % in pension phase) and new loan establishment costs. Refinancing Refinancing to a new lender or better rate is permitted provided the new loan meets all LRBA requirements and is used solely to repay the original LRBA. You cannot "top up" the loan to extract equity for other investments; doing so converts the arrangement into a general borrowing, which is banned under Section 67(1) SIS Act. Most lenders will require a fresh bare trust deed or deed of variation, and your SMSF auditor will expect documented evidence that no additional funds were drawn for non-LRBA purposes. The sole-purpose test and related-party leases Every decision—acquisition, financing, leasing, sale—must satisfy the sole-purpose test in Section 62 SIS Act: the fund must be maintained solely to provide retirement benefits to members (or their dependants if a member dies). Two common LRBA scenarios raise red flags: Buying property from a related party An SMSF can acquire residential property from a related party using an LRBA, but only if: The purchase is at arm's-length market value , supported by an independent valuation. The transaction is for the sole purpose of building retirement benefits, not to bail out a member's personal cash-flow crisis. The LRBA loan itself is from an unrelated lender or, if from a related party, on commercial terms and documented by a solicitor. Section 66 SIS Act prohibits acquiring assets from related parties in many cases, but residential property is carved out under Reg 13.22C SIS Regulations 1994 , provided it meets market-value and sole-purpose tests. The ATO has disqualified funds where members sold personal homes to their SMSF at inflated prices to access super early. Leasing the property to a member You cannot lease residential property held in your SMSF (whether under an LRBA or owned outright) to a fund member or relative under Section 66 SIS Act. Business real property —offices, warehouses, retail shops— can be leased to a related party at market rent, and this is a popular structure for members who own trading companies and want to hold commercial premises in super. If you lease commercial property held under an LRBA to your own company, ensure: Rent is set at market rate , reviewed annually. A formal lease agreement is executed and stamped where required. The SMSF maintains adequate cash reserves to cover loan repayments if the tenant (your company) defaults. Your SMSF auditor will test whether the lease terms would be accepted by an unrelated landlord. Preferential rent or informal handshake leases can trigger non-compliance penalties. How lenders assess SMSF borrowing capacity Unlike a personal home loan, there is no household income to assess. Instead, lenders evaluate: 1. Current fund balance and liquidity post-settlement. 2. Member demographics : age, employment status, years to retirement. 3. Projected contributions : concessional (employer super, salary sacrifice) and non-concessional. For FY 2024–25, concessional caps are $27,500 per member per year; non-concessional caps are $110,000 (or up to $330,000 under bring-forward rules if total super balance is below $1.9 million). 4. Rental income from the property (discounted by a vacancy factor, typically 5–10 %). 5. Pension obligations : if members are already in pension phase, projected minimum drawdowns (4–14 % per annum depending on age) reduce serviceability. 6. Existing investments : dividend and distribution income from shares or managed funds. Most SMSF lenders use a debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR) hurdle of at least 1.25× , meaning projected annual income must exceed annual loan repayments by 25 %. This is more conservative than many personal investor loans. Interest-only terms of 5–10 years are common, reverting to principal-and-interest. Some lenders will extend interest-only periods if the fund's balance is growing and members are far from retirement. Costs, stamp duty and the in-specie transfer LRBA transactions carry higher upfront and ongoing costs than personal property loans: Loan establishment fees : $1,000–$3,000, sometimes higher for commercial property. Bare trust deed : $800–$2,000 from a specialist SMSF lawyer. Lender legal costs : often $500–$1,500 passed on to the borrower. Valuation : $300–$800 for residential, $2,000+ for commercial. Ongoing admin : the SMSF must track loan interest (deductible), principal repayments (not deductible), depreciation, and any capital works separately in the fund's accounts. Stamp duty is payable on acquisition at the prevailing state rates. The SMSF (as beneficial owner) pays duty at the investor/non-first-home rate; no concessions apply. In Victoria, a $600,000 apartment incurs roughly $31,070 duty (as at 2025 rates). When the loan is repaid and legal title transfers from the bare trust to the SMSF trustee, most states exempt or heavily discount duty on the in-specie transfer because beneficial ownership never changed. Always notify the state revenue office and lodge the appropriate form to claim the exemption; automatic processing is rare.
