Rent Increase Notice Rules by State | InvestorJoint + Lease · 19 Nov 2026 · 9 min read Rent Increase Notice Rules by State Every Australian state sets different notice periods, frequency limits, and fixed-term restrictions for rent increases. Here's what landlords and property investors need to know before issuing a notice. Every Australian state and territory enforces different rules about how much notice you must give a tenant before increasing rent, how often you can do it, and whether you can increase rent during a fixed-term lease. As of 2024, notice periods range from 30 days in Tasmania to 60 days in New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, Western Australia, South Australia, and the ACT. Most jurisdictions ban rent increases during fixed-term leases unless the agreement explicitly allows it, and several states now require at least 12 months between increases. If you issue a notice that doesn't comply with your state's rules, it's void—and the tenant can dispute it through the tribunal. Why rent increase rules differ across Australia Residential tenancy law is a state responsibility, not federal. That means each state and territory legislature sets its own notice periods, frequency caps, and fixed-term rules. Over the past five years, most jurisdictions have tightened their rental legislation in response to tight vacancy rates and affordability pressure—Victoria introduced a 12-month minimum gap between increases in 2021, Queensland followed in 2022, and the ACT did the same in late 2023. The policy intent is straightforward: give tenants enough time to budget or negotiate, and prevent landlords from using frequent small increases to push tenants out. From an investor's perspective, these rules introduce compliance friction but also reduce turnover risk. A tenant who receives proper notice at a lawful frequency is less likely to lodge a tribunal application or vacate abruptly. New South Wales: 60 days, 12 months apart Notice period: 60 days Minimum gap: 12 months from the last increase or the start of the tenancy Fixed-term rule: No increase during a fixed term unless the lease explicitly states the timing and amount (or a formula) New South Wales moved to a 60-day notice period on 23 March 2020 and introduced the 12-month frequency cap on the same date. Before that, the standard was 60 days with no minimum gap. Today, if a tenant moved in on 1 July 2023, the earliest you can issue a rent increase notice is 1 May 2024 (60 days before 1 July 2024). If you increased rent on 1 July 2024, the next increase cannot take effect before 1 July 2025. For fixed-term leases, the Residential Tenancies Act 2010 (NSW) s 41 allows an increase only if the agreement specifies when and by how much—or includes a formula tied to CPI or another index. A generic clause such as "rent may be reviewed annually" is not enough; you must state the new rent or the method. If you issue a notice that breaches the 60-day or 12-month rule, the tenant can apply to the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal (NCAT) to have it set aside. The tribunal has the power to declare the notice void and order the tenant to continue paying the old rent. Stamp duty on residential investment properties in NSW ranges from 1.25 % to 5.5 % depending on the purchase price; you can model acquisition costs with the NSW stamp duty calculator . Victoria: 60 days, 12 months apart Notice period: 60 days Minimum gap: 12 months Fixed-term rule: No increase during a fixed term unless the lease sets out the amount or method in advance Victoria amended the Residential Tenancies Act 1997 on 29 March 2021 to introduce a 12-month minimum gap between increases. Before that, landlords could increase rent as often as every six months (with 60 days' notice). The reform aligned Victoria with NSW and several other jurisdictions. The 60-day notice requirement applies to both periodic and fixed-term leases. During a fixed term, you can increase rent only if the lease document states the new rent or a formula—for example, "Rent will increase by CPI on each anniversary." A clause that says "Rent may be increased at the landlord's discretion" does not meet the statutory test. If a fixed-term lease is silent on rent increases, you must wait until the lease rolls over to a periodic tenancy or negotiate a new fixed term with the tenant. At that point, the 12-month clock resets from the most recent increase or the start date of the original lease. Victorian landlords often ask whether they can include a stepped rent schedule in the lease—say, $500 per week for the first six months, then $525 for the second six months. Consumer Affairs Victoria's guidance states that a schedule agreed at lease signing is permitted, because it's not a unilateral "increase" under the Act; it's a term the tenant accepted. However, any further increase after the schedule ends must observe the 60-day and 12-month rules. Queensland: 60 days (periodic), two months before expiry (fixed-term) Notice period (periodic): 60 days (changed from two months effective 1 July 2023) Notice period (fixed-term): At least two months before the lease ends Minimum gap: 12 months (since 1 July 2023) Fixed-term rule: No increase during the term unless the agreement states the amount or method Queensland overhauled its tenancy laws in stages. The 12-month frequency cap took effect on 1 July 2023, replacing the previous six-month rule. At the same time, the notice period for periodic leases switched from "two months" to a fixed 60 days, removing ambiguity over whether "two months" meant 60, 61, or 62 days depending on the calendar. For fixed-term leases, the Residential Tenancies and Rooming Accommodation Act 2008 (Qld) requires at least two months' notice if you plan to increase rent at the end of the term and roll into a new fixed term or periodic tenancy. If you want to increase rent during a fixed term, the lease must specify the new rent or the method (for example, annual CPI adjustment). Practically, this means that if you have a 12-month lease ending on 31 December 2024, you must give notice by 31 October 2024 if you want the new rent to take effect on 1 January 2025. If you miss that window, you can still issue notice once the lease becomes periodic, but you'll need to give 60 days. The Residential Tenancies Authority (RTA) is Queensland's bond holder and dispute mediator. The RTA publishes quarterly median rents by postcode; many landlords reference these figures when deciding how much to increase. Keep in mind that the tribunal (QCAT) has the power to set aside an increase if it finds the new rent "excessive"—though this is rare and typically requires evidence of market comparables. Stamp duty on Queensland investment property runs up to 5.75 % for non-owner-occupiers. Use the QLD stamp duty calculator to estimate upfront costs. Western Australia: 60 days, six months apart Notice period: 60 days Minimum gap: Six months Fixed-term rule: No increase during a fixed term unless the lease specifies the new rent or method Western Australia stands out for retaining a six-month minimum frequency rather than 12 months. The Residential Tenancies Act 1987 (WA) requires 60 days' written notice and allows an increase every six months from the most recent increase or the start of the tenancy . The practical effect is that a WA landlord can lift rent twice as often—in dollar terms—as a landlord in NSW or Victoria, provided each increase complies with the 60-day notice rule. This flexibility is politically contentious; consumer advocates have called for a 12-month gap, but as of mid-2024 the government has not amended the legislation. During a fixed term, you can increase rent only if the lease document states the amount or a formula. If the lease is silent, you must wait until it rolls to periodic or negotiate a new agreement. WA's Department of Mines, Industry Regulation and Safety oversees tenancy disputes through the Magistrates Court. Unlike some eastern states, WA does not have a dedicated tenancy tribunal; disputes are heard in the magistrates' jurisdiction. For stamp duty planning, Western Australia charges up to 5.15 % on residential transfers. The WA stamp duty calculator breaks down the marginal rates. South Australia: 60 days, 12 months apart Notice period: 60 days Minimum gap: 12 months Fixed-term rule: No increase during a fixed term unless the lease specifies the amount or method South Australia amended the Residential Tenancies Act 1995 (SA) in 2020 to introduce a 12-month minimum gap between increases and extend the notice period from 60 days to—wait, it was already 60 days. The key change was the frequency cap: before 2020, landlords could increase rent every six months; now it's annual. The notice must be in writing and given at least 60 days before the increase takes effect. The South Australian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (SACAT) can set aside a notice that breaches the timing rules or is "excessive" compared to market rent for comparable properties. For fixed-term leases, an increase is permitted only if the lease states the new rent or a CPI-linked formula at the time of signing. A generic review clause is insufficient. Stamp duty in South Australia peaks at 5.5 % for high-value residential property. The SA stamp duty calculator provides a breakdown by bracket. Tasmania: 30 days (periodic), 60 days (fixed-term renewal) Notice period (periodic): 30 days Notice period (fixed-term renewal): 60 days Minimum gap: No statutory minimum Fixed-term rule: No increase during a fixed term unless the lease specifies Tasmania has the shortest notice period in Australia for periodic tenancies—just 30 days under the Residential Tenancy Act 1997 (Tas). However, if you want to increase rent at the end of a fixed term and transition to a new fixed term or periodic lease, you must give at least 60 days' notice. There is no statutory minimum gap between increases in Tasmania. In theory, a landlord could issue a 30-day notice, wait 30 days, and issue another. In practice, frequent increases invite tribunal scrutiny and tenant turnover. The Residential Tenancy Commissioner and the Tasmanian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (TasCAT) have the power to declare a rent "excessive" if it's significantly above market. During a fixed term, you cannot increase rent unless the lease document specifies the amount or a method. The legislation is clear: a unilateral increase mid-term without prior agreement is void. Stamp duty in Tasmania runs up to 4.5 %. The TAS stamp duty calculator covers all brackets. Australian Capital Territory: 60 days, 12 months apart Notice period: 60 days Minimum gap: 12 months (since 23 November 2023) Fixed-term rule: No increase during a fixed term unless the lease specifies The ACT joined NSW, Victoria, Queensland, and South Australia in requiring a 12-month gap between rent increases when amendments to the Residential Tenancies Act 1997 (ACT) took effect on 23 November 2023. Before that date, the minimum was six months. The 60-day notice period applies to both periodic leases and fixed-term renewals. During a fixed term, an increase is lawful only if the lease sets out the new rent or a formula at signing. The ACT Civil and Administrative Tribunal (ACAT) can set aside an increase that breaches the notice or frequency rules, or is "unjustifiably high" relative to market rent. ACAT publishes anonymised decisions, and several recent cases have turned on whether the landlord gave exactly 60 days or miscounted weekends. Stamp duty in the ACT has been progressively reduced as part of a long-term shift toward land tax. For investment property, the top marginal rate is around 4.54 %. Northern Territory: 30 days, no statutory frequency limit Notice period: 30 days Minimum gap: No statutory minimum Fixed-term rule: No increase during a fixed term unless the lease specifies The Northern Territory mirrors Tasmania in having a 30-day notice period and no legislated frequency cap. The Residential Tenancies Act 1999 (NT) requires written notice at least 30 days before the increase takes effect. Because there's no minimum gap, an NT landlord can—in theory—issue consecutive 30-day notices. However, the NT Civil and Administrative Tribunal (NTCAT) has jurisdiction to hear disputes about "excessive" rent, and frequent increases that push rent well above comparable properties can be challenged. During a fixed term, you cannot increase rent unless the lease document states the amount or method in advance. This is consistent across all jurisdictions. Stamp duty in the NT reaches 4.95 % at the top bracket. The NT stamp duty calculator will show the rate schedule.
