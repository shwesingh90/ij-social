Refinancing Investment Property Equity Release | InvestorJoint + Finance · 20 Nov 2026 · 9 min read Refinancing an Investment Property: Releasing Equity Without Tangling Your Tax Releasing equity from an investment property can unlock capital for your next purchase—but how you structure the refinance determines whether the interest stays deductible. If your investment property has grown in value, refinancing to release equity can fund your next deposit, renovations, or even pay down private debt. But the ATO cares what you use the borrowed funds for—not where they came from—and mixing purposes inside a single loan account is the fastest way to lose tax deductions you're entitled to. The golden rule: keep your investment-purpose debt separate from personal-purpose debt by using split loan structures, and document every dollar at the time you draw it down. Why equity release matters for multi-property investors Suppose you bought an investment property in Brisbane in 2019 for $550,000 with a $440,000 loan. By mid-2024 it's worth $750,000, leaving you with roughly $310,000 in usable equity at 80 % LVR. That equity is dead capital unless you refinance or sell. Refinancing lets you extract cash without triggering capital gains tax, stamp duty, or agent commissions. Most investors release equity to fund: Deposits on the next investment property , keeping the acquisition cycle moving. Renovations or capital improvements to the existing rental (which may also lift depreciation schedules). Debt consolidation , paying off credit cards, car loans, or the family home mortgage. Each of those uses has a different tax outcome. The ATO's position—set out in Taxation Ruling TR 2023/1 (which consolidated earlier rulings including TR 95/33 and TR 2000/2)—is that interest deductibility follows the purpose of the borrowing, not the security. If you borrow against an investment property to buy another investment property, the interest on that additional borrowing is deductible. If you borrow against an investment property to renovate your family home, it isn't. The split-loan structure: your first line of defence A split loan divides your total facility into two or more separate loan accounts, each with its own balance, interest rate, and redraw or offset. When you refinance to release equity, ask your broker or lender to structure the new facility so that: 1. Loan A covers the original investment-property debt (or the amount still owing on the property's purchase and any prior deductible purposes). 2. Loan B is the new equity-release tranche, used only for the new purpose—say, the deposit on Property 2. By keeping these balances in separate accounts, your lender's statements will show exactly which interest payments relate to which purpose. Come tax time, you deduct 100 % of the interest on Loan A and Loan B (assuming both are investment-related), and there's no apportionment headache. If you don't split, and you simply increase your existing loan by $150,000 for a new deposit, all future repayments (principal and interest) blend across the old and new purposes. The ATO expects you to apportion interest on a "reasonable basis"—usually the ratio of outstanding balances—but lenders don't track this automatically, leaving you to reverse-engineer it every quarter or rely on your accountant's spreadsheet. That complexity compounds when you make extra repayments or redraw. How to keep deductible and private debt separate Even with a split loan, you can still tangle your tax if you: Redraw funds from an investment loan to pay for a private expense (a holiday, school fees, home renovations). The ATO treats redraws as new borrowings; if the purpose is private, you lose the deduction on that portion going forward. Use an offset account funded by mixed sources . Offset accounts themselves don't have a tax character—they simply reduce the interest charged on the linked loan. But if you deposit rental income, salary, and a tax refund into the same offset, then draw it out for a mix of purposes, tracing becomes difficult. Best practice: link your investment-loan offset to your investment income stream only, and keep private cash in a separate account. A concrete example: you refinance your Sydney investment property and release $200,000 into Loan B to fund a deposit on a Melbourne apartment. Twelve months later you have $30,000 sitting in Loan B's redraw, and you pull it out to renovate your family bathroom. That $30,000 is now private-purpose debt; interest on it is non-deductible. If you don't split it into Loan C, you'll need to apportion Loan B's interest every year for the life of the loan. The cleaner approach: open a new loan split (Loan C) for the $30,000 bathroom renovation, and leave Loan B untouched. Your statements will show three splits—original property debt, Melbourne deposit, bathroom reno—each with a clear purpose. Lender serviceability and the refinance approval process Releasing equity increases your total debt, so lenders re-assess your serviceability before approving the new limit. In 2024, APRA's 3 % interest-rate buffer (applied on top of the actual rate) and the removal of COVID-era forbearance mean that borrowing capacity is tighter than it was in 2020–21. Lenders calculate serviceability on: Net rental income after applying a 20–30 % "haircut" for vacancy, maintenance, and management (even if your property has never been vacant). Your other investment-property debts , which reduce uncommitted monthly income. PAYG or business income (if you're self-employed, most lenders average the last two years' tax returns). Living expenses , benchmarked to the Household Expenditure Measure (HEM) or your actual declared expenses, whichever is higher. If you hold properties in a family trust or company, the trust's or company's debt still affects your personal serviceability if you've given personal guarantees—which almost every lender requires. Interest-only versus principal-and-interest: Many investors keep investment loans interest-only to maximise cash flow and deductions, then pay down the family home (non-deductible debt) faster. If you're releasing equity and the new tranche will be used to acquire another investment, keeping it interest-only is usually tax-efficient. Just remember that lenders typically cap interest-only periods at five years, after which the loan reverts to P&I and your repayments jump. Documenting the purpose at drawdown The moment you sign the refinance discharge authority and your solicitor (or the lender) disburses the new funds, you need a paper trail showing where every dollar went. The ATO can ask for evidence years later—especially if they audit your rental deductions—and "I think it was for the deposit" won't cut it. Best-practice documentation: 1. Loan split schedule from your lender, showing the facility limit and purpose annotation for each split. 2. Drawdown authority or settlement statement that specifies the recipient (e.g., the vendor's solicitor for Property 2, or your own cheque account if you're staging the funds). 3. Bank statements showing the funds moving from the new loan account to the investment purpose—conveyancer trust account, builder's invoice, or another property loan. If you're releasing equity to pay off an existing investment loan (debt recycling within the portfolio), keep both the old and new loan statements so your trail is unbroken. If you're consolidating private debt—say, paying off a $40,000 car loan—that $40,000 slice is non-deductible, and you should isolate it in its own split from day one. One edge case: if you draw down the equity and leave it in a redraw or offset "for now," intending to use it for an investment purpose later, the interest is not deductible until you actually deploy the funds for that purpose. The ATO's position is that merely having access to money, or even earmarking it mentally, doesn't satisfy the nexus test. Deploy the funds, then claim the deduction. Refinancing costs and their tax treatment Refinancing isn't free. Typical costs include: Discharge fees from your old lender ($150–$500). Application and valuation fees for the new lender ($600–$1,000 combined). Lender's Mortgage Insurance (LMI) if your new LVR exceeds 80 % (can be $5,000–$20,000+ on a large loan). Legal or conveyancing fees if you're switching lenders and need to register a new mortgage ($800–$1,500). Break costs if you exit a fixed-rate loan early (highly variable; can be $0 or $10,000+ depending on rate movements). Under section 25-25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997, borrowing expenses —including LMI, application fees, and loan establishment costs—are deductible over five years (or the term of the loan if shorter) when the loan is used to produce assessable income. If your refinance is purely for investment purposes, all of these costs go into your five-year deduction pool. If it's a mixed-purpose refinance, you apportion the costs by the ratio of investment debt to total debt. Example: You refinance a $600,000 investment loan and release an extra $100,000 for a family-home renovation. Total new facility: $700,000. You pay $3,500 in establishment fees and $8,000 in LMI. The investment portion is $600k ÷ $700k = 85.7 %. You can deduct 85.7 % × $11,500 = $9,856 over five years—roughly $1,971 per year. Discharge fees on the old loan are usually deductible in the year you pay them, provided the old loan was itself fully deductible. Using redraw as a slush fund. Investors see $50,000 available in redraw and think "that's my money, I already paid it off." But if you paid extra into an investment loan and then redraw for a private purpose, you've just created non-deductible debt. The ATO's view: you could have kept those extra payments in an offset (preserving flexibility) or in a separate savings account. 2. Cross-collateralising without splits. Some lenders offer a single loan secured against multiple properties. You refinance Properties A and B together and draw out $200,000 for Property C. Unless the lender (and you) maintain sub-accounts or splits showing which portion of the debt relates to A, B, and C, apportioning interest becomes a nightmare. Always insist on separate loan accounts or at least annotated splits. 3. Failing to update depreciation schedules. If you release equity to renovate or extend the investment property, any new capital works (structural improvements, fixed assets) qualify for depreciation under Division 43 and Division 40. Claiming those deductions requires an updated quantity surveyor's report. Many investors forget to order one, leaving thousands in deductions on the table. If the rent on the original property doesn't cover the new, larger loan—and you haven't yet settled the next purchase—you can find yourself funding a gap out of after-tax salary. State-by-state stamp duty on refinance (usually nil) Good news: refinancing an existing loan with the same lender or a new lender, even if you increase the amount, typically attracts no stamp duty on the mortgage in any Australian state, provided the security (the property or properties) doesn't change hands. Mortgage duty has been abolished in all states except the Northern Territory, which still charges 0.15 % of the loan amount on new mortgages as of July 2023. If you're releasing equity to buy a new property, that purchase will attract stamp duty (transfer duty) in the state where the new property is located. Those rates vary significantly—compare using InvestorJoint's state-specific calculators: Stamp duty NSW Stamp duty VIC Stamp duty QLD Stamp duty WA Stamp duty SA The key planning point: factor the stamp duty on the new purchase into your total equity requirement. A $200,000 release might look like enough for a 20 % deposit on a $900,000 property—until you remember that NSW transfer duty on $900k is roughly $35,000, and QLD is about $32,000. What this means for your portfolio Refinancing to release equity is one of the most powerful levers in a multi-property portfolio—but only if you protect the tax deductions and maintain a clear audit trail. Every time you increase a loan, ask three questions: 1. What am I using this money for? (Investment or private.) 2. Is this new borrowing in its own split or account? 3. Do I have paperwork showing the funds went to that purpose? If you can answer all three confidently, you'll keep your deductions intact and avoid apportionment headaches for the next decade.
