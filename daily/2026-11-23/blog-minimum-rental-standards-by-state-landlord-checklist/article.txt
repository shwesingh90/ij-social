Minimum Rental Standards by State: A Landlord Checklist | InvestorJoint + Lease · 18 Nov 2026 · 9 min read Minimum Rental Standards by State: A Landlord Checklist Every Australian state mandates different baseline standards for rental properties. Here's what you must provide in NSW, Victoria, Queensland, and beyond before you sign a lease. If your rental property doesn't meet minimum standards, the tenant can serve a breach notice, withhold rent, or terminate early—and your insurer may refuse a claim. Each state sets its own baseline for smoke alarms, heating, security, ventilation, and mould prevention, and the bar has risen sharply since 2020. This checklist breaks down the law in every jurisdiction so you know exactly what must be in place before you hand over the keys. Why minimum standards matter more than ever Between 2020 and 2024 every mainland state overhauled rental minimum standards, driven by COVID ventilation concerns, energy-efficiency targets, and a wave of Tribunal cases around mould. Victoria led the charge with 68 legislated minimums from March 2021, and other states followed with their own lists. Fail to comply and you face: Breach notices that can shorten your lease term. Rent reduction orders backdated to the start of the breach. Repair orders with tight deadlines, enforceable through Tribunal. Insurance gaps : many landlord policies exclude damage arising from non-compliant fixtures (for example, an electrical fire caused by a double adaptor in lieu of sufficient power points). If you manage through an agent, the agent is not liable for your property's physical compliance—you are. The onus sits with the owner to deliver a premises that meets the legislated floor. Smoke alarms and fire safety (all states) Every jurisdiction requires photoelectric smoke alarms (not ionisation) installed according to Australian Standard AS 3786. The number and location vary, but the common rule is: One on every storey. One in every bedroom or within three metres of bedroom doors (some states allow "near bedrooms" rather than inside). Hardwired or ten-year lithium battery; replaceable nine-volt batteries are progressively banned. Victoria's March 2027 deadline applies to all existing rental stock; non-compliant properties after that date cannot be lawfully leased. Fire extinguishers and blankets are not mandated for standard residential tenancies (they are required in rooming houses and certain HMO setups), but installing them in the kitchen and laundry improves your insurer's view. Heating (Victoria, ACT—and practical expectations elsewhere) Victoria is the only state with a legislated heating minimum: every rental must have a fixed heater in the main living area capable of heating that room to at least 20 °C when the outside temperature is 0 °C. Bar heaters, fan heaters, and underfloor electric slab warmers do not count. Central ducted, split-system reverse-cycle, gas space heaters, or flued wood heaters all satisfy the rule. ACT requires premises to include "suitable heating," but no specific wattage or BTU output is prescribed; Tribunal precedent suggests the main living room must have a fixed appliance, not a portable radiator. Other states do not mandate heating, but case law in NSW and Queensland has found that a property which becomes so cold in winter that condensation and mould develop may breach the implied warranty of fitness for habitation. In practice, most landlords in temperate and cool climates now install reverse-cycle split systems to meet tenant expectations and avoid mould disputes. Cooling is not mandated anywhere except for "suitable ventilation" clauses (see below). In northern Queensland and Darwin, most leases include air conditioning as a fixture, but it is not strictly required by law—only that the property can be ventilated to prevent mould. Security: locks, doors, and windows Every state requires secure entry points . The baseline is: Deadlocks (not just key-in-knob locks) on external doors. Window locks on accessible ground-floor windows and any window reachable from a balcony or stairwell. Screen doors and windows that latch securely (where installed). Victoria's minimum standards specify: Deadlocks complying with AS 4145.1 (Class 1 or 2) on external doors—simple barrel bolts do not count. Window locks on all accessible windows (ground floor or reachable by climbing); sliding and louvre windows must have key locks or pin locks. NSW, Queensland, and South Australia have similar rules, though the standard is less prescriptive; Tribunal cases often cite AS 4145 as the benchmark even where the legislation does not name it. Security screens and grilles are not mandated, except that if they are present they must function correctly and not obstruct emergency egress. If you remove old security doors, ensure the underlying door meets the deadlock requirement. Garage remotes and intercom fobs : supply at least two of each at the start of the lease. If the property has swipe-card entry or digital locks, confirm the tenant receives two functioning credentials. Mould prevention: ventilation and dampness This is the fastest-moving area of rental law. Since 2020 Tribunal members have issued dozens of rent-reduction and termination orders citing mould and poor ventilation as breaches of the habitability standard. Legislative triggers Victoria : Minimum Standard 50 requires "adequate natural or mechanical ventilation" in kitchens and bathrooms to minimise dampness. Where there is no window, an exhaust fan vented to outside is compulsory. NSW : The Residential Tenancies Act 2010 s 52(1)(c) requires the premises to be "fit for habitation," which Tribunal case law now interprets as including effective ventilation to prevent mould. The Public Health Act 2010 s 118 allows councils to issue orders for mould remediation. Queensland : The tenancy must be "fit for the tenant to live in" (s 185 RTRA Act 2008 ). Poor ventilation leading to black mould has been accepted as grounds for rent reduction backdated up to twelve months. Other states : Similar habitability or "reasonably clean and fit" clauses exist; mould is treated as a maintenance failure if it arises from landlord-controlled defects (blocked weepholes, roof leaks, broken extractor fans). Practical checklist 1. Bathroom exhaust fan (if no window): vented to outside, not just into the ceiling cavity. Test that it runs and check the duct terminates through the eaves or roof. 2. Kitchen exhaust or rangehood : vented models are strongly preferred; recirculating charcoal filters reduce odour but do not remove moisture. 3. Laundry : external door or window, or exhaust fan if internal laundry. 4. Weepholes clear at the base of brick veneer walls. 5. Downpipes and gutters functional and not leaking into wall cavities. 6. Ceiling and wall cracks sealed to prevent warm moist air entering the roof space and condensing on sarking. 7. Window sills and frames painted and sealed—bare timber absorbs moisture. Before taking progress photos of any existing mould for your records before tenants move in; if mould appears during the lease, you can demonstrate whether it pre-dated the tenancy or arose from tenant behaviour (drying laundry indoors with windows shut, etc.). Water efficiency and plumbing Landlords pay water supply charges ; tenants typically pay usage only if the property meets water-efficiency criteria: NSW, VIC, QLD, ACT : All internal taps and shower heads must have a minimum WELS (Water Efficiency Labelling and Standards) rating—3-star for taps, 3-star for showers—and toilets must be dual-flush or low-flush. If you do not meet these standards, tenants cannot be billed for usage. WA, SA, TAS, NT : Most leases pass usage costs to the tenant if the property has a separate meter, but specific WELS rules are less strict; check local water corporation by-laws. Hot water systems must deliver hot water within a reasonable time. Victoria prescribes that if the system fails, emergency repair must commence within 24 hours. In practice, install a system sized to the number of bedrooms (a 50-litre electric tank for a one-bedroom unit will breach the minimum in most Tribunal views; 125 litres or larger for two-bedroom, 250+ for four-bedroom). Leaking taps, toilets, pipes : The landlord must repair these promptly. A slow-drip tap can waste 20,000 litres per year; if the tenant is billed for usage, they can dispute the bill and claim reimbursement for water wasted by landlord-controlled fixtures. Energy efficiency: windows, draught-sealing, and disclosure Victoria mandates: Window coverings (blinds, curtains, or shutters) in living rooms and bedrooms to reduce heat loss. Draught-sealing of key gaps around doors and windows. These minimums became compulsory on 29 March 2021 for new leases and 29 March 2024 for all tenancies. Other states do not (yet) mandate window coverings, but the market increasingly expects them. Bare windows in winter contribute to condensation and mould, triggering the habitability issue. Energy-efficiency disclosure From 1 May 2024 Victoria requires landlords to include the property's energy-efficiency rating (0 to 10 stars) on all new leases, if a certificate has been obtained. Rating certificates are not compulsory for rental properties, but once you have one you must disclose it. By 1 May 2026 it is expected (though not yet legislated) that properties below 2 stars may not be leased. No other state currently mandates energy-efficiency disclosure for residential rentals (commercial leases must disclose NABERS ratings in some jurisdictions), but the Nationwide House Energy Rating Scheme (NatHERS) is being extended; watch for similar rules in ACT and NSW by FY 2026. Kitchens, bathrooms, and essential services Minimum standards generally require: Stove or cooktop (at least two burners or hobs) and oven in working order. Some states allow a tenancy to be advertised without cooking appliances if disclosed upfront, but the market norm is built-in stove and oven. Kitchen sink with running hot and cold water. Bathroom with shower or bath, basin, toilet; all with hot and cold water. Laundry tubs or washing-machine taps —or access to a common laundry in a strata building. Electricity connected and switchboard compliant with current standards (safety switches / RCDs on power circuits and lights in Victoria from 2024; other states lag but the Australian Consumer Law expects "electrically safe" premises). Adequate power points : Victoria prescribes minimums (one double power outlet per bedroom, two in living areas, etc.). Other states rely on "fit for habitation," but Tribunal members have ordered landlords to add GPOs where the tenant must run extension boards for essential appliances. Light fittings : At least one fixed light fitting (ceiling or wall-mounted) in every habitable room and hallway. Bayonet or Edison-screw sockets must accept standard bulbs; GU10 downlights are acceptable if bulbs are provided. The tenant is not required to supply their own ceiling fixtures. Floor coverings : Carpets must be clean and in reasonable condition; worn carpet that harbours odours or allergens has been found to breach habitability in several cases. Floorboards and tiles must be level and free of trip hazards. Stamp duty and compliance timing when you acquire a tenanted property If you buy an investment property with an existing tenant in place, you inherit the obligation to bring the premises up to minimum standards—but the law usually gives you a transition period . For example: Victoria : If you purchased before 29 March 2021, smoke alarms and heaters must comply by 29 March 2027 (six-year grace period). If you bought after March 2021, the property must comply before the first new lease. NSW : The 1 March 2024 smoke-alarm deadline applied to all rentals, including tenanted premises at settlement. Queensland : The 1 January 2022 smoke-alarm deadline meant any property acquired after that date had to be compliant at handover. Your solicitor should include a special condition in the Contract of Sale requiring the vendor to deliver the property compliant or to provide a dollar allowance for you to complete works. Otherwise, you wear the cost—and the tenant can serve a breach notice immediately after settlement if the outgoing landlord left the property non-compliant. Miss one exhaust fan in Queensland and you risk a rent reduction backdated six months; fall short of Victoria's March 2027 smoke-alarm deadline and your agent cannot legally re-let the property.
