Trust vs Individual Property Ownership Tax Compared | InvestorJoint + Structure · 26 Nov 2026 · 9 min read Trust vs Individual Ownership: Tax and Asset Protection Compared Family trusts offer asset protection and income splitting, but individuals get the full CGT discount and simpler land tax. Here's how the tax treatment differs and when each structure makes sense. If you're buying your second or third investment property in Australia, the choice between holding it in your own name or inside a family trust carries permanent tax and asset-protection consequences. Trusts offer income splitting and creditor insulation but trigger land-tax aggregation in most states and complicate borrowing; individuals enjoy the full 50% CGT discount and cleaner financing but expose the asset to personal liability. The right answer depends on your marginal tax rate, the state where you buy, and whether you work in a sue-prone profession—and you can't easily reverse the decision once settlement occurs. How rental income is taxed: individual vs trust When you hold an investment property in your personal name, rental income and expenses flow straight onto your individual tax return. Net rental losses reduce your taxable income at your marginal rate—potentially 47% (including Medicare Levy) if you earn above $180,000 in FY 2024–25—making negative gearing a powerful cash-flow subsidy in the early years. A discretionary family trust treats rental profit or loss differently. The trust itself pays no tax; instead, the trustee distributes income each year to nominated beneficiaries—your spouse, adult children, or even back to you—and each pays tax at their own marginal rate. If your partner has little other income, the first $18,200 of trust distributions is tax-free, the next slice taxed at 19%, and so on. That flexibility can shave thousands off the family tax bill when rents exceed expenses. However, trust losses are quarantined: they remain trapped inside the trust and can only offset future trust income. You cannot stream a $15,000 rental loss to your personal return to shelter your salary. This makes trusts cash-flow unfriendly during the typical negative-gearing phase unless you have other positive trust income—say from a trading business or a second property—to absorb the red ink. Capital gains tax: the 50% discount difference Individuals who hold an asset for at least twelve months receive a 50% CGT discount under Division 115 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997. Sell a property for a $400,000 gain, and only $200,000 is added to your assessable income. At the top marginal rate that saves roughly $94,000 in tax. Trusts also receive the 50% discount on long-term gains, but the benefit shrinks when distributions flow to beneficiaries. If the trustee distributes a discounted capital gain to an individual beneficiary, that person still enjoys the discount. But distribute to a company beneficiary—common in integrated structures—and the company is taxed on the full gain at 25% or 30% with no discount at all. Many practitioners use a trust to hold property and distribute income widely, then stream capital gains specifically to individual beneficiaries to preserve the concession, but the ATO watches distribution minutes closely under TR 2012/D1 and PS LA 2012/5. One edge case: if you're a non-resident for tax purposes when the CGT event happens, you forfeit the 50% discount entirely, whether you own personally or via trust. The ATO applies the discount only to Australian residents under section 115-105. Land tax: aggregation traps in every state This is where trust ownership becomes expensive. Every mainland state aggregates all land holdings within the same trust when calculating land tax, while properties in your personal name sit in a separate bucket—and in some states enjoy a higher threshold. Victoria (State Revenue Office Victoria, Land Tax Act 2005) gives individuals a tax-free threshold of $50,000 in total land value for 2024, then charges 0.2% on the next $50,000 up to progressively steeper rates. A single trust gets the same $50,000 threshold but all properties in that trust are lumped together. If you own two Melbourne apartments in one trust with combined site values of $1.2 million, the entire amount above $50,000 is taxable. Buy one in your name and one in a trust, and each enjoys its own threshold—potentially saving $2,000–$5,000 a year. Check Victoria's current rates with the stamp duty calculator for Victoria and remember that land tax is an annual impost, not a one-off. New South Wales (Revenue NSW, Land Tax Management Act 1956) uses a $1,039,000 threshold for individuals in 2024 and a separate $1,075,000 threshold for trusts, but aggregates all NSW land in a single trust. If you're building a portfolio of three Sydney units under one discretionary trust, you'll hit the trust threshold faster than if each sat in a different ownership bucket. Queensland (Queensland Revenue Office, Land Tax Act 2010) starts land tax at a combined site value of $600,000 for individuals and applies a lower $350,000 threshold to companies and trusts. Many Brisbane investors discover a $4,000 annual land-tax bill that wouldn't exist if the same two properties were held personally. South Australia and Western Australia have similar aggregation rules; in WA the threshold is $300,000 and trusts often pay land tax from day one on metropolitan property. Tasmania and the ACT also aggregate trust holdings. The workaround—establishing multiple trusts for separate properties—adds $1,500–$3,000 per year in accounting and trustee fees, so the land-tax saving must exceed the administration cost. Asset protection: why high-risk professions favour trusts If you're a surgeon, director, or run a business with trade creditors, holding investment property in your personal name exposes that asset to any judgment or bankruptcy. A plaintiff who wins a negligence suit can place a caveat on your personally owned investment property and force a sale to satisfy the debt. A properly settled discretionary trust—where you are not the appointor or sole beneficiary—places legal ownership with the trustee (often a $2 corporate trustee) and beneficial ownership with the class of discretionary beneficiaries. Creditors of an individual beneficiary generally cannot reach trust assets, provided the trust wasn't settled to defeat existing creditors (which would be a voidable transaction under the Bankruptcy Act 1966). The asset protection is not absolute—courts can set aside sham arrangements, and family-law property settlements often look through trust structures—but it creates a meaningful barrier. Professionals in high litigation-risk roles often accept the land-tax aggregation cost as the price of shielding a $2 million property portfolio from a single adverse judgment. Salaried employees with stable employers and no personal trading risk usually conclude the protection benefit doesn't justify the complexity. Borrowing and serviceability: banks charge more for trusts Lenders treat trust borrowers as higher risk. Mortgage interest rates for a loan to XYZ Family Trust as borrower—with individual guarantees from the beneficiaries—are typically 0.10–0.30 percentage points higher than for a loan in your personal name. That difference compounds over thirty years: an extra 0.20% on a $600,000 loan costs roughly $37,000 in additional interest over the life of the loan. Serviceability is also tighter. Banks assess rental income at a discount (usually 80% of market rent) and apply the higher of your actual interest rate or a floor rate around 3.00–3.50 percentage points above the loan rate. When the trust has quarantined losses from other properties, the lender consolidates all trust liabilities and may demand that high-income beneficiaries personally guarantee the entire trust debt. If you later want to refinance or draw equity, the trustee must consent and all guarantors must sign again, turning a straightforward refinance into a week-long paperwork trail. Personal ownership keeps the loan documentation simple and the approval process faster. When to choose individual ownership Individual ownership suits you if: 1. You're a salaried employee with no professional indemnity risk and no business trading debts. 2. You're buying in a high land-tax state (Queensland, Western Australia, South Australia) and want to preserve separate thresholds for each property. 3. Your marginal tax rate is already high and your spouse earns a similar income, so income splitting delivers minimal benefit. 4. You plan to sell within ten years and want the simplicity of the full 50% CGT discount flowing straight to your return. 5. You value clean financing and want the lowest possible interest rate and fastest refinance path. A Sydney couple both earning $120,000, buying a second investment unit in their joint names, will enjoy negative-gearing relief at a combined 39% marginal rate (including Medicare Levy), face no land tax until their combined NSW holdings exceed $1,039,000, and each keep the 50% CGT discount on their half-share when they eventually sell. The structure is simple, the annual compliance cost is nil, and their PAYG withholding adjusts automatically to reflect the rental loss. When to choose trust ownership A family trust makes sense if: 1. You're a business owner, director, or professional in a litigation-exposed occupation (medical, construction, financial advice). 2. You have a spouse or adult children on low incomes who can receive trust distributions below the $45,000 tax bracket, shifting income from your 47% marginal rate to their 19% or 32.5% rate. 3. You're acquiring multiple properties quickly and want centralised control with flexible distribution, even though land tax will aggregate. 4. You expect the portfolio to turn cash-positive within three years , so quarantined losses are a short-term nuisance rather than a long-term drag. 5. Estate planning matters : trusts let you control asset succession through the appointor role and deed amendments, rather than relying solely on a will that must pass through probate. A Melbourne orthodontist earning $350,000 with a spouse at home and two adult children at university can establish a discretionary trust, distribute $60,000 of annual rental profit across three low-income beneficiaries to utilise their tax-free thresholds, and shield a $3 million property portfolio from any malpractice judgment. The circa $4,500 annual land-tax impost and $2,500 trust accounting fee are tolerable costs for the tax arbitrage and asset ring-fencing. Before you settle on a trust, model the land-tax aggregation in your state and confirm your lender's trust rate and guarantee requirements. Stamp duty and ongoing compliance costs Stamp duty on acquisition is identical whether you buy as an individual or as trustee for a family trust in every Australian state. The transfer attracts duty on the same dutiable value under state revenue legislation; the entity structure is irrelevant. (If you later transfer a property into a trust post-settlement, most states charge duty again as a fresh conveyance—a costly trap.) Annual compliance differs sharply: Individual ownership : rental schedule appended to your personal tax return; your accountant charges $150–$400 to prepare it. Trust ownership : separate trust return (around $800–$1,500), plus corporate trustee ASIC annual review ($59 in 2024), plus trustee resolutions and distribution minutes. Total annual cost $1,200–$2,500 for a simple trust with two properties. Over a ten-year hold, that compliance gap compounds to $10,000–$20,000. Switching structures: the CGT and duty sting Changing your mind is expensive. Transferring a property from your name into a trust is a CGT event A1—you are deemed to have disposed of the asset at market value, triggering capital gains tax on the accrued gain, even though no money changed hands. If the property has appreciated $200,000, you'll pay tax on a $100,000 gain (after the 50% discount), potentially $47,000 at the top rate. Stamp duty applies again in every state. Moving a $900,000 property in NSW costs roughly $34,000 in duty; in Victoria about $49,000. Those transaction costs usually exceed any future land-tax or asset-protection benefit unless you're genuinely at imminent litigation risk. The lesson: choose your ownership structure before you exchange contracts. Once settlement occurs, you're locked in for the economic life of the asset. Depreciation and capital works: no structural difference Both individuals and trusts claim the same capital works deductions (Division 43, 2.5% per year on post-1987 construction costs) and plant and equipment depreciation (Division 40) under identical rules. The deductions flow through to the same tax return—yours personally, or the trust's—and reduce taxable rental income dollar-for-dollar. The only wrinkle: if the trust has quarantined losses, the depreciation benefit is deferred until the trust turns profitable. In contrast, an individual gets the cash-flow benefit immediately via PAYG withholding variation. Ordering a depreciation schedule before settlement ensures you're claiming every available deduction, whichever structure you choose. What this means for your portfolio Trust-versus-individual is not a one-size decision. If you're salaried, buying in NSW or Victoria, and both spouses earn similar incomes, personal ownership delivers lower land tax, simpler borrowing, and full CGT flexibility with minimal compliance overhead. If you're self-employed, earning well above $180,000, and your spouse stays home with young children, a discretionary trust can save $10,000–$15,000 a year in tax through income splitting—even after the land-tax and accounting impost—and shield your portfolio from business creditors.
