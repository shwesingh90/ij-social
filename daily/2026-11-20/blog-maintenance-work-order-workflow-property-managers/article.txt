A Maintenance Work-Order Workflow That Property Managers Can Run + Pm · 18 Nov 2026 · 6 min read A Maintenance Work-Order Workflow That Property Managers Can Run The stages of a good maintenance work-order lifecycle, where agencies lose time between them, and how to run it in one system from request to ledger. A good work-order workflow takes a maintenance problem from the tenant's first message to a paid invoice on the owner's statement, with every step visible to everyone involved. Most agencies do the steps; what they lose is the time between them, when a quote sits in an inbox or an approval is waiting on a text message. The fix is less about doing more and more about keeping the whole thread in one place. The lifecycle, step by step Whatever software you use, a healthy work order moves through the same stages. | Stage | What happens | What should be recorded | |---|---|---| | Request | Tenant reports an issue, ideally with photos and a short description. | Who, when, which property, photos, how urgent. | | Triage | The property manager decides urgency and whether it needs a trade at all. | Priority, notes, who is handling it. | | Quote | A vendor inspects and quotes, or minor work proceeds without a quote. | The quote document, the amount, the vendor. | | Owner approval | The owner approves spend above their threshold. | Who approved, when, and what they saw. | | Vendor engaged | The approved vendor is booked and told what is expected. | Booking, access arrangements, contact details. | | Completion | Work is done and confirmed. | Completion date, photos or notes. | | Invoice | The vendor invoices; the invoice is checked against the quote. | The invoice itself, attached to the job. | | Paid and ledgered | The vendor is paid and the expense lands in the books and the owner statement. | Payment date, the ledger entry. | Two points about this list. Triage is where good agencies earn their keep: some requests are emergencies, some are routine, and some are not the owner's cost at all. The rules on urgent repairs, who can authorise them and the timeframes involved are set by each state's tenancy law, so check your state regulator's guidance rather than relying on a general rule. And approval thresholds are normally something the owner and agency agree up front, so that a $50 callout does not need a conversation but a $4,000 job does. Where agencies lose time The pattern shows up in the same places across rent rolls of every size. Requests that arrive by phone, text and email. A tenant texts about a leaking tap. Unless someone turns that into a record, it exists only in one person's phone. Quotes that live in inboxes. The quote arrives as an email attachment. Finding it three weeks later, when the owner asks what they approved, takes longer than the repair did. Approvals by text. "Yep go ahead" from an owner is an approval, but it is hard to find, hard to prove and invisible to anyone else on the team. Chasing vendors for status. Without a shared status, the question "has the plumber been?" becomes a phone call to the plumber, then the tenant, then the plumber again. Invoices that arrive late and miss the statement. A job completed in March is invoiced in April. If nobody connects it back to the work order, it misses the owner statement or lands without explanation. Paid invoices that never reach the books. The trade is paid, but the expense sits in a spreadsheet instead of the ledger that the owner statement and end-of-year reports read from. Each of these is a handoff between two tools or two people. The more handoffs a job crosses, the more places it can stall. What to look for in work-order software A short test for any system you are evaluating: 1. Can a tenant log a request with photos without calling you? 2. Is there one status per job that the manager, owner and tenant can all see? 3. Does the owner approval happen inside the job record, not in a separate message? 4. Can minor, pre-approved work skip the quote step? 5. Do the quote and invoice attach to the job? 6. Does marking the job paid create the expense in the ledger automatically? If the answer to the last question is no, you are re-keying every invoice, and that is where errors and missed deductions come from. How InvestorJoint's workflow handles it InvestorJoint's maintenance workflow follows the lifecycle above, in one system alongside the rent roll and the books. From the product's own documentation: A full lifecycle. A job moves through open, quoted, accepted, in progress, completed, invoiced and paid, visible to the property manager and owner. Tenants see the status of the work linked to their request, without the vendor or the price. Tenant requests with photos. Tenants can log a request with photos from their phone and track its status through the tenant portal, rather than phoning the office. A per-job owner-approval step. The manager chooses for each job whether owner approval is required. When it is on, the job cannot start until the owner approves, and the approval stays attached to the job. Vendors invited directly. A vendor directory lets you invite specific vendors to quote a particular job, with ratings from past work, instead of chasing by phone. A vendorless quick path. Minor work with a price agreed up front can skip the quote step, so a small callout does not force a full quote cycle. Owner approval still applies if it is switched on for the job. Paid means ledgered. Marking a work order paid creates the matching expense and posts it to the ledger, so it reaches the owner statement without a separate bookkeeping step. If the owner's trust balance is short, the job cannot be marked paid until the shortfall is requested from the owner. Quotes and invoices stay with the job. The contractor's quote and invoice are attached to the work order, so the thread stays with the property instead of dying in a text message. A work order is the record of a maintenance job: what needs doing, who is doing it, what it was quoted at, who approved it and what it finally cost. It is the single place the whole job is tracked.
