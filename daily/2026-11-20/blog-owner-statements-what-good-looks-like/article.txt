Owner Statements: What a Good One Shows and Why It Must Foot + Pm · 18 Nov 2026 · 7 min read Owner Statements: What a Good One Shows and Why It Must Foot What an owner statement should show, the errors that erode owner trust, and why the net must foot to the dollar. A practical guide for Australian agencies. An owner statement is the one document that turns a month of rent, fees and repairs into something an owner can check in two minutes. A good one shows money in, every deduction, the net paid out and what is still held, and it foots: the figures add up to the dollar without the owner needing to ask. If it does not foot, nothing else on the page matters, because the owner stops trusting the rest of it. What an owner statement should show Owners are not accountants. They want to know three things: what came in, what was taken out and why, and what they were paid. A statement that answers those without a phone call has done its job. | Section | What it should contain | |---|---| | Income | Rent received per period, with the date received, plus any other receipts (for example a rent-related charge recovered from a tenant). | | Management fees | Each fee as its own line, with the basis of the calculation (a percentage of rent, a fixed amount, a letting fee). | | Expenses | Every repair, invoice and outgoing deducted, each with a date, payee and description, and the supporting invoice. | | Net paid | The amount actually paid to the owner, the date, and the account it went to. | | Balance held | Any money still held for the owner, and why (for example a pending invoice or a retained reserve). | Two details separate a useful statement from a merely complete one. First, the description of each expense should be written for the owner, not the agency: "Plumber, leaking tap, 14 Smith St" rather than a supplier code. Second, the opening and closing positions should connect from one period to the next, so an owner can read two consecutive statements and see that the closing balance on one is the opening balance on the next. Why it has to foot to the dollar "Foots" is the old bookkeeping word for adding up. Income, less fees, less expenses, equals net paid, plus or minus movement in the balance held. Every line should be traceable and the arithmetic should close with no unexplained difference. This matters for more than neatness. Rent and owner money in a property management agency normally sits in trust, and the statement is the owner-facing view of that ledger. A statement that does not reconcile to the trust ledger is a signal that something is wrong in the records, even if the cause is small. Requirements differ by state, so confirm the detail with your state regulator, but the principle is the same everywhere: you should be able to show where every dollar of an owner's money went. For the reconciliation side of this, see our month-end reconciliation checklist . An unfooted statement is also expensive in time. One owner emailing "this doesn't add up" can cost an hour of digging through the ledger, and the answer is often an expense that was deducted but not listed. Common errors that cost owners' trust These are the patterns that most often make a statement fail to foot or fail to convince: A deduction that is missing from the listed fees. The owner net is lower than rent less the visible fees, because an invoice or other deduction was netted off without its own line. An expense with no invoice behind it. The owner sees "Repairs $380" and has no way to check it. Statements that change after they are sent. An owner keeps a PDF, a later correction silently alters the ledger, and now the two disagree. Mixed periods. Rent received on the last day of the month lands in the next statement, so income looks short against the lease. GST and fee treatment hidden inside a total. Fees shown only as a combined figure make it impossible to check the percentage. Co-owned properties. The statement shows whole-property gross and fees but only one owner's net, with no line explaining the share paid to the others, so it appears not to add up. Almost all of these come from the same root: the statement is assembled by hand, or from a report that is separate from the ledger, so it can drift from it. A short checklist before you send any statement 1. Does income less fees less expenses equal the net paid? 2. Is every deduction listed on its own line, with a description an owner would understand? 3. Is there an invoice or receipt behind every expense? 4. Does the opening balance match the previous closing balance? 5. For a co-owned property, is the share paid to the other owners explained? 6. If something was corrected, is the correction visible rather than silent? If your current process cannot answer yes to all six without manual checking, the fix is usually in how the statement is produced, not in more careful proofreading.
