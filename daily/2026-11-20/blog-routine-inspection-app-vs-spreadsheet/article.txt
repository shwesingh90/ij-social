Routine Inspections: Property Inspection App vs Spreadsheet + Pm · 18 Nov 2026 · 6 min read Routine Inspections: Property Inspection App vs Spreadsheet Paper and spreadsheet inspections compared with a phone inspection app, what a good inspection record contains, and what to check before you switch. A phone inspection app beats paper and spreadsheets on speed, evidence and follow-through: notes and photos are captured in the room, stamped with the time, and filed against the property without retyping. Paper and spreadsheets still work for a single property and a tenant you trust. The deciding factors are how many inspections you do, and how often you need to prove what a property looked like on a given day. What a routine inspection is for A routine inspection checks the condition of the property, spots maintenance before it becomes expensive, and confirms the tenant is looking after the place. How often you can inspect, how much notice you must give and how the inspection is conducted are set by state and territory tenancy law, so confirm the rules for your property with your state tenancy authority before you plan a schedule. Whatever the schedule, the inspection is only as useful as the record it leaves behind. Paper and spreadsheet inspections Many landlords and small agencies start here, and it is a reasonable place to start. What works Free, familiar and flexible. You can design the checklist exactly as you like. No app to learn and nothing to log into. Where it breaks down Photos live in a phone gallery, separate from the notes. Handwritten notes must be typed up later, and some never are. A photo's date depends on the phone, and it is easy to lose which room it came from. Follow-up items sit on a page instead of becoming a job someone owns. Finding last year's inspection means finding last year's folder. Owners hear about it later, if at all. A phone inspection app An app moves the whole record to the point of capture. You walk the property with a phone, tick items, type or dictate a note for each area, take photos in place, and the record is saved against the property when you finish. What it improves Notes are taken room by room rather than reconstructed afterwards. Photos are tied to the inspection and carry a timestamp. The record is searchable and sits with the lease and maintenance history. A repair found at an inspection can be raised as a maintenance job from the same system. The report can be sent to the owner when the inspection is done. What it does not do It does not replace judgement. A photo only helps if someone took the right photo. It does not remove the legal requirements around notice and conduct. It needs a phone signal or an offline plan; ask any vendor how it behaves in a basement or a lift lobby. Side by side | Factor | Paper or spreadsheet | Phone inspection app | |---|---|---| | Set-up cost | Nil | Free to low, depending on product | | Capturing notes | Handwritten, typed later | Entered in the room | | Photos | Separate gallery | Attached to the inspection | | Timestamp on photos | Whatever the phone holds | Recorded with the inspection | | Finding old inspections | Folder search | Property file | | Follow-up repairs | Manual | Can be raised as a job in the same system | | Owner visibility | Email or phone call | Report can be emailed on completion | | Best for | One property, a stable tenancy | Several properties, or any dispute risk | What a good inspection record contains Whichever tool you use, a record that will hold up when someone asks "what did it look like?" has these parts: 1. Date, time and who attended , including whether the tenant was present. 2. The property and lease it belongs to. 3. Area-by-area notes. Entry, living areas, kitchen, bathrooms, bedrooms, laundry, outdoor areas, each with its own condition note. 4. Photos for each area , with a timestamp, taken in a consistent order so a later photo can be matched to an earlier one. 5. Specific, neutral wording. "Hairline crack in the bathroom ceiling, 20 cm, no staining" beats "ceiling poor". 6. Items needing action , with who is responsible and a date. 7. A copy to the people who need it , according to your state's rules. How to decide Stay with paper or a spreadsheet if you have one property, a long and settled tenancy, and an inspection a year that you are happy to type up. Move to an app if any of these are true: You manage several properties and the typing-up is slipping. You report to owners who expect a prompt, consistent record. You have had, or fear, a disagreement over condition. You want repairs found at an inspection to be raised as maintenance jobs in the same system. If you are weighing a switch, run one real inspection in the app next to your usual method and compare the two records. The guide to rental inspection reports explains how to write the report itself, whichever tool you choose. FAQ How often should I do a routine inspection? It depends on your state or territory, which sets limits on frequency and notice. Check with your state tenancy authority rather than relying on a general rule. Are timestamped photos enough evidence? They help, but they are one part of a record. Pair them with area-by-area notes, and use the same photo order every time so changes are easy to see. Can I use a spreadsheet and still keep good photos? Yes, if you name and store photos consistently, for example by property, date and room. It takes discipline, and an app does the filing for you. Does an app compare move-in and move-out photos automatically? On InvestorJoint it does not. The per-space inventory on incoming and end-of-lease inspections can be compared item by item, but photo-level comparison is not built. Ask any vendor to show this feature live before you rely on it.
