CGT Main Residence Exemption Rental: Six-Year Rule | InvestorJoint + Tax · 12 Nov 2026 · 8 min read Renting Out Your Home: How the CGT Main Residence Exemption Changes When you rent out your principal place of residence, the CGT main residence exemption doesn't vanish instantly—but the clock starts ticking on the six-year rule and your cost base may reset. The moment you advertise your principal place of residence on a rental portal, capital gains tax treatment changes—but you don't automatically forfeit the main residence exemption. Instead, the ATO starts a timer: you can be absent for up to six years while the property earns income and still claim a full or partial exemption when you sell, provided you meet strict continuity and election rules. Miss a detail—such as the cost-base reset that applies when a property is first used to produce income —and you can hand the ATO tens of thousands of dollars in CGT you didn't need to pay. The main residence exemption in a nutshell Under Division 118 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 , your principal place of residence is ordinarily exempt from capital gains tax. That means if you bought a house for $600,000 in 2015, lived in it continuously, and sold it in 2025 for $1,200,000, the $600,000 gain is entirely tax-free—no CGT, no 50 per cent discount needed. Three conditions underpin the exemption: 1. The dwelling must be your principal place of residence (the ATO looks at where you sleep, where your mail arrives, where your family lives, and where utilities are connected). 2. You must not have claimed another property as your main residence for the same period. 3. The land must be two hectares or less, unless the excess land is necessary for the reasonable enjoyment of the dwelling. The trouble begins when you move out and lease the property. That introduces a period of absence during which the dwelling produces assessable income, and partial CGT exposure becomes the default outcome. The six-year absence rule explained Subdivision 118-B contains the "absence rule" that most landlords misunderstand. If you move out of your home, you can treat it as your main residence for CGT purposes for up to six years —even if it is rented—provided: the dwelling was your main residence immediately before you moved out; you do not elect another property as your main residence during that period; and the property is not being used to produce income before it became your main residence (the "first used to produce income" trap, discussed below). Critically, the six-year clock resets each time you move back in and re-establish the property as your principal place of residence. If you move back for three months and then leave again, a fresh six-year period begins. Example: Clara bought an apartment in South Melbourne in July 2018 for $520,000 (stamp duty paid in Victoria using the stamp duty calculator ). She lived there until March 2021, when her employer posted her to Sydney. She rented the apartment from April 2021. In March 2027—six years later—she sells for $680,000. Because the absence was under six years and she elected no other main residence, the $160,000 gain is fully exempt under s 118-145. If Clara had held on until May 2027, seven months past the six-year mark, she would apportion her exemption: roughly 87 per cent of the ownership period qualifies (72 months main residence + 72 months absence-exemption = 144 months of 157 total), and 13 per cent is taxable. The taxable slice of $160,000 is approximately $20,800; with the 50 per cent CGT discount, Clara's assessable gain is $10,400. The "first used to produce income" trap Section 118-192 contains a nasty sting: if you acquire a dwelling and first use it to produce income before you move in, the six-year absence rule does not apply. Instead, only the period you actually occupy the property as your main residence qualifies for the exemption. This most often catches investors who: buy off-the-plan and rent the unit out for 12 months before moving in; inherit a property, lease it immediately, then later move in; purchase an interstate property, install tenants, and plan to relocate "eventually." Example: Ben bought a townhouse in Brisbane in February 2020 for $580,000 and immediately leased it. In August 2022 he relocated from Melbourne and lived in it until December 2024, then moved to Perth for work and re-leased it. When Ben sells in 2028, he can only claim the main residence exemption for the 28 months he actually lived there (August 2022–December 2024). The six-year absence rule is unavailable because the property was first used to produce income. If Ben had moved in for any period before the first tenant arrived—even a fortnight—he would have avoided the trap and unlocked the six-year rule for future absences. The ATO's position is found in Taxation Ruling TR 98/5 and more recent guidance in PCG 2021/4, which deals with working-from-home arrangements but reinforces the principle that "first use" is determined by objective facts: key collection, utility connection, and whether rent was ever charged before personal occupancy. Cost-base reset and the market-valuation election When you convert a main residence into an income-producing asset, s 118-192(2) gives you an election to reset your cost base to the market value on the day the property is first used to produce income. This is an explicit, irrevocable choice. If you make it: your original purchase price and associated acquisition costs (stamp duty, conveyancing) are disregarded; the new cost base becomes the property's market value at the date of first rental income; and you lose the main residence exemption for the period before the property was first rented. Why would anyone elect a reset? If the property appreciated strongly while you lived in it, resetting the cost base to a higher number can reduce your taxable gain when you eventually sell. The trade-off is that you forfeit the full exemption for the pre-rental period. Example: Saanvi bought a house in Hobart in April 2015 for $410,000. She lived there until June 2020, when she moved to Canberra and began renting the property. Market value in June 2020: $620,000. She sells in October 2024 for $710,000. Without the election: Total gain $300,000 ($710k – $410k). Ownership 9.5 years; main residence for 5.2 years, rental 4.3 years. Exempt portion ≈ 55%, taxable ≈ $135,000. With 50% discount, assessable gain ≈ $67,500. With the cost-base reset election: New cost base $620,000. Total gain $90,000. Entire gain is taxable (no main-residence period after first income use), but with 50% discount, assessable gain = $45,000. The election must be made in your tax return for the year the CGT event happens (the sale). Practically, you need a formal valuation at the date the property was first let; most accountants will ask for a retrospective desktop appraisal from a qualified valuer, which will cost $300–600. State revenue offices also care about this election during land-tax assessments: once a dwelling is income-producing, it loses the principal-place-of-residence land-tax exemption in New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia, and Tasmania. Queensland does not levy land tax on a single holding below the threshold, but the exemption disappears once the property is rented. Partial exemptions and the apportionment formula When the six-year rule runs out or you never moved back in, you calculate the exempt portion by comparing days the property was your main residence (including the absence period covered by the rule) against total days of ownership . The ATO formula in TR 2001/4 is: $$ \text{Exempt %} = \frac{\text{Days as main residence} + \text{Days of exempt absence}}{\text{Total days owned}} $$ Multiply your capital gain by (1 – Exempt %) to find the taxable slice, then apply the 50 per cent CGT discount if you held the asset longer than 12 months. Worked example: Liam owned a unit in Canberra from 1 July 2016 to 30 June 2025 (3,287 days). He lived there until 30 June 2019 (1,096 days), then rented it out. The six-year absence exemption covers 1 July 2019–30 June 2025 (2,191 days). Total exempt days: 3,287 → 100% exempt. If Liam sold on 1 January 2026 instead (3,471 total days), his exempt portion is 3,287 ÷ 3,471 ≈ 94.7%. The remaining 5.3% of the gain is taxable. Moving back in: resetting the clock and CGT planning The simplest way to preserve full exemption is to move back into the property for a genuine period before the six years expire, then move out again if needed. There is no minimum re-occupation period in the legislation, but the ATO expects a bona fide change of residence: furniture moved, mail redirected, utilities in your name, and evidence you slept there most nights. A weekend visit or keeping a spare toothbrush will not suffice. In FCT v Benson [2011] FCA 1370, the Federal Court found that a taxpayer who occasionally stayed at a property while it was tenanted had not re-established it as a main residence. Strategic re-occupation is common among Defence personnel, fly-in-fly-out workers, and expatriates who know they will be offshore or interstate for more than six years. Moving back for six months every five years can preserve the exemption indefinitely, provided the property genuinely becomes your principal place of residence each time. What this means for your portfolio Renting out your former home is not a binary event for CGT: partial exemptions, cost-base elections, and the six-year rule create a spectrum of outcomes.
